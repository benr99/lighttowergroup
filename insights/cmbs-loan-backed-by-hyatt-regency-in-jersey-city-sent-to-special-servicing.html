CMBS Loan Backed by Hyatt Regency in Jersey City Sent to Special Servicing

A $100 million CMBS loan secured by a 351-key waterfront Hyatt Regency in Jersey City has been transferred to special servicing ahead of an expected maturity de

Ben Rohr September 16, 2026 2 min read 1 source
Share LinkedIn X / Twitter

A $100 million commercial mortgage-backed securities loan secured by the 351-key waterfront Hyatt Regency hotel in Jersey City, N.J., has been sent to special servicing because of an imminent maturity default, according to an alert from Morningstar Credit Analytics. The transfer matters because the loan backs portions of three conduit CMBS deals — CGCMT 2016-P5, CGCMT 2016-P6, and CMBX.10 — meaning the distress could flow through to bondholders across multiple securitizations. The sponsorship team has stated it will be unable to pay off the loan ahead of its October 2026 maturity, setting up a resolution process that Morningstar Credit expects to take the form of a loan extension or forbearance.

The property at 2 Exchange Place opened in 2002 and traded for $117 million in December 2022, when Taconic Capital Advisors and HEI Hotels & Resorts purchased the Hyatt Regency from Veris Residential and Hyatt Hotels. The hotel sits on a pier directly on the Hudson River Waterfront Walkway in Downtown Jersey City and features more than 20,000 square feet of event space, a fitness center, a self-parking garage, and an on-site restaurant. It is a five-minute walk from a New Jersey PATH train with access into Manhattan. Despite those locational advantages, the asset's operating performance has lagged its debt underwriting for nearly the entire life of the loan.

The evidence from the Commercial Observer report shows persistent cash flow shortfalls. Since the 2016 origination of the three CMBS loans, the Hyatt Regency has had only one year — 2016 — when its net cash flow met the levels underwritten on the debt. The hotel reported negative cash flow in 2024, and its net income came out 68 percent less than its underwritten expectations in 2025. Those figures, drawn from Morningstar Credit Analytics, indicate that the maturity default is not a sudden shock but the culmination of a multiyear underperformance trend. The dossier does not provide occupancy, average daily rate, or revenue per available room figures, so the analysis cannot attribute the shortfall to specific demand or expense drivers.

For the commercial real estate and CMBS market, the special servicing transfer is a reminder that hospitality loans originated in the mid-2010s can still face maturity pressure even when located in strong urban submarkets. The loan's presence in CMBX.10 also means the distress could be relevant to investors tracking synthetic commercial real estate credit indexes, though the dossier does not quantify the loan's share of any deal or index. Morningstar Credit's expectation of an extension or forbearance suggests the special servicer may pursue a consensual resolution rather than an immediate foreclosure, but no terms or timeline are provided in the source material.

The main limitation of this analysis is that it rests on a single full-text source, a Commercial Observer article dated September 15, 2026. The dossier does not include the underlying Morningstar alert, servicer commentary, borrower financial statements, or loan documents. Taconic Capital Advisors did not respond to requests for comment, according to the report. What to watch next is whether the special servicer executes an extension or forbearance before the October 2026 maturity, whether the sponsorship contributes new capital, and whether any modification changes the loan's interest rate, amortization, or reserve requirements. Until those details emerge, the observable facts point to a maturity default driven by years of underwhelming net cash flow at a well-located but underperforming waterfront hotel.
