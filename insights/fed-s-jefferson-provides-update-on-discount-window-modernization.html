Fed’s Jefferson provides update on discount window modernization

Fed Vice Chair Philip Jefferson outlined operational changes to the discount window, including a self-service portal now handling more than 60% of loan requests

Ben Rohr September 23, 2026 2 min read 1 source
Share LinkedIn X / Twitter

Federal Reserve Vice Chair Philip Jefferson provided an update on the central bank’s efforts to modernize the discount window, the facility that provides short-term credit to banks and credit unions. The update matters because the discount window is a core liquidity backstop for depository institutions, and operational friction can deter its use precisely when funding stress emerges. Jefferson said the Fed has taken steps in recent years to improve business operations, enhance user experience, and boost collaboration with the Federal Home Loan Banks so that banks can access liquidity more quickly.

The modernization effort has focused on practical mechanics. Jefferson said the Fed has introduced simplified forms, faster enrollment, automated pledged loan lists, and centralized resources so institutions can more easily find what they need. A key milestone was the 2024 launch of Discount Window Direct, a self-service online portal. According to Jefferson, that portal now accounts for more than 60% of discount window loan requests. The Fed also issued a request for information in 2024 about the discount window’s operational practices and used that feedback, along with other outreach, to shape the changes.

The evidence comes from a single secondary source, the ABA Banking Journal, which summarized Jefferson’s speech in New York City. The report quotes Jefferson directly on the discount window’s stabilizing role: “By providing banks with a reliable source of liquidity, the discount window serves as a shock absorber during periods of market stress by reducing the risk of forced sales of Treasury securities.” He added that the facility’s influence extends to broader money markets, helping to alleviate funding pressures at depository institutions and supporting the smooth functioning of the repurchase agreement market and the federal funds market. The source does not provide a full transcript or additional independent corroboration, so the analysis is bounded by that account.

For banks and credit unions, the operational changes could lower the administrative burden of maintaining discount window access and reduce hesitation around using the facility in a stress scenario. The emphasis on faster enrollment and automated pledged loan lists is notable because collateral readiness is often a practical barrier to quick borrowing. The collaboration with Federal Home Loan Banks also suggests an effort to create smoother back-end arrangements between two liquidity channels that depository institutions may use in different circumstances. For Treasury market participants, Jefferson’s framing connects the discount window to reduced forced selling of government securities, which has implications for market functioning during volatility.

The main limitation is that the dossier offers no independent data on borrowing volumes, pricing, or the actual speed improvements achieved. It also does not specify how many institutions have enrolled in Discount Window Direct or whether the more than 60% share of loan requests reflects broad adoption or concentration among a subset of users. What to watch next is whether the Fed publishes additional operational metrics, whether stigma concerns are addressed through further outreach, and whether the FHLB collaboration produces measurable reductions in the time required for institutions to pledge collateral and receive funds.
