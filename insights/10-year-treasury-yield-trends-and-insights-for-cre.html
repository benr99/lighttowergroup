10-Year Treasury Yield Trends and Insights for CRE

The 10-year U.S. Treasury yield has climbed to 5.34%, and the 30-year to 5.70%, the highest marks since 2002, after both rose at least seven basis points on renewed pressure Monday. That matters for commercial real estate because the 10-year is a widely recognized gauge of long-term interest rates and economic expectations, and its yield directly influences financing costs, property values, and overall market activity. A year ago the 10-year sat at 4.13%, and the quarter that just ended produced the largest quarterly increase in the 10-year yield this century. For an industry that has spent two years waiting for rates to come back down, the bond market appears to have moved on from that expectation.

The mechanics of the move are revealing. Shorter maturities climbed only two to four basis points while the long end sold off, which means the market is not repricing Federal Reserve policy. It is repricing something the Fed does not control. When the front of the curve holds steady while the back end sells off, the signal is about long-term supply, inflation expectations, and fiscal risk rather than near-term rate decisions. The clearest evidence came last Friday, when a weaker than expected jobs report failed to move yields down. Soft labor data is supposed to pull the 10-year lower, but it closed higher instead. A bond market that no longer rallies on bad economic news is telling investors that growth is not what is driving the price.

Several forces are pushing in the same direction. Federal debt was 56% of GDP in 2002, the last time yields were at these levels, and it is now roughly 120%, with every basis point adding to the interest expense that has to be refinanced into the same market doing the selling. Energy prices have climbed with the Strait of Hormuz closed, feeding inflation expectations at exactly the wrong moment. Japan's 10-year is at its highest level since 1996, which means the cheap capital that flowed out of Tokyo and into Treasuries for two decades is staying home. France's spread over German bunds hit a 14-year wide last week. Treasury has tried to lean against the move by doubling its long-bond buybacks from $2 billion to $4 billion in August, and Secretary Bessent has signaled a willingness to deploy considerably more. The 30-year has risen roughly 20 basis points since, suggesting intervention has not changed the trajectory.

For commercial real estate, the practical consequence is that the refinancing math most deals were underwritten against no longer applies. A 2021 or 2022 vintage loan coming due assumed a 10-year somewhere in the threes. At 5.34%, the gap gets covered with fresh equity, a sale, or an extension nobody wants to give. Cap rates have been slower to adjust than the debt markets, and the longer the 10-year holds above 5%, the harder that disconnect becomes to sustain. The source material does not provide transaction-level data or specific cap rate movements, so the exact pace of repricing remains an open question. What is clear is that the direction of long-term rates has shifted, and CRE stakeholders are now operating against a financing backdrop that no longer matches the assumptions embedded in recent vintage loans.

The evidence here comes from a single full-text source, Propmodo, which cites recent values published by the Federal Reserve and describes market moves without additional corroborating datasets. The article does not include granular detail on Treasury auction results, foreign official flows, or commercial mortgage delinquency rates, so those channels cannot be independently assessed from this material. What to watch next is whether the 10-year holds above 5%, whether Treasury expands buybacks further, and whether cap rates begin to move more decisively toward the levels implied by the debt markets. If the bond market has indeed moved on, the commercial real estate market may not be far behind.
