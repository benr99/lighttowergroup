The $369.5M Refi Built to Run Five Separate Clocks

PGIM closed the final $82.6 million bridge loan on a 1,340-unit New Jersey portfolio, leaving Capodagli free to refinance each asset on its own timeline.

The building at 10 West Westfield Avenue is not the biggest apartment complex in New Jersey. It is the last one that mattered for a $369.5 million refinancing.

Capodagli Property Company has closed an $82.6 million bridge loan from PGIM on Meridia Roselle Park 10, a 325-unit Class A property in Roselle Park, N.J., 25 miles southwest of Times Square. The loan, arranged by Greystone Capital Advisors' Drew Fletcher, Bryan Grover, Miryam Reinitz-Kops and Jesse Kopecky, completes a debt program that stretched across five New Jersey assets and 1,340 units.

Here is the math. PGIM had already put $287 million of cross-collateralized bridge financing on four Capodagli properties in 2025: Meridia Village Commons in South Orange, Meridia Pompton Lakes, Meridia Linden 1001, and Meridia Little Ferry. Add the Roselle Park loan and the portfolio total reaches $369.5 million. The Roselle Park piece covers 325 of the 1,340 units, leaving the other four assets with the larger share of the debt.

The more revealing detail is not the size but the structure.

Greystone's Fletcher said Capodagli broke the cross-collateralized facility into separate deals so each asset could be refinanced 'on consistent terms as each came online,' with flexibility to seek permanent financing 'at its own rate.' That is not a throwaway quote. It describes a sponsor running five separate refinancing clocks instead of one portfolio-level maturity.

The decision makes sense. A single $369.5 million permanent loan on five different New Jersey towns would mean one rate, one maturity, one lender relationship, and one negotiation. Separate bridge loans mean Capodagli can take the first stabilized asset to a permanent lender when the market is better, without waiting for the rest. The cost is that bridge debt is not permanent. Each loan still has to be taken out.

What the disclosed figures omit are the terms that will determine the refinancing pressure. The bridge rate, term, LTV, and DSCR on Roselle Park do not appear in the source. Per-unit loan amounts are arithmetic from reported figures, not lender underwriting. If the other four assets carry more debt per unit than Roselle Park, that may reflect unit mix, rent levels, or land basis—not which property will face the harder refinancing conversation later.

The last $82.6 million was not the hard part. The hard part is what happens when the bridge clock starts running.

The market test is whether any of the five assets gets a permanent loan in the next twelve to eighteen months, and at what spread. If Capodagli finds takeout financing one asset at a time, the structure worked. If the bridge terms start maturing and the sponsor is still looking, PGIM's portfolio conviction will be tested one building at a time.
