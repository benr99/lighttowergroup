The Fusion Industry Needs $10 Billion to Beat China

Commonwealth Fusion Systems CEO Bob Mumgaard says the U.S. risks losing its lead in the race to commercialize nuclear fusion without roughly $10 billion in new

Ben Rohr September 24, 2026 3 min read 1 source
Share LinkedIn X / Twitter

Without billions in new federal investment, the United States may lose its pole position in the global race to become the first nuclear fusion superpower, Commonwealth Fusion Systems CEO Bob Mumgaard told attendees at Heatmap House in New York City. The warning matters because fusion is being framed not merely as a clean energy moonshot but as a strategic technology contest with China, one Mumgaard compared to the race for dominance in artificial intelligence. When asked whether Commonwealth Fusion could still develop its fusion aspirations at scale without U.S. government financing, Mumgaard said, “I think so – it’s a question of the timing and the place,” before suggesting the company and the industry might go elsewhere if the country does not put more capital into the sector.

The material stakes are substantial. The Massachusetts-based company raised $1 billion in new investment just a couple of months before the remarks, and it is aiming to put electrons onto the grid by the 2030s. Yet Mumgaard said a Fusion Industry Association request to Congress and the Trump administration for $10 billion of investment might be what is needed for that power to be American first. “We debated that [amount] with the industry association, and you have to say what gets the job done. It’s a disservice to lowball what’s needed,” he said. “This is a very important thing. It’s an entirely new industry. Let’s treat it as such.”

The evidence comes from a single Heatmap News report covering Mumgaard’s onstage appearance at New York Climate Week. The source notes that innovation in nuclear power is broadly popular in Congress, which has an influential bipartisan Fusion Energy Caucus, and that Commonwealth has received public support from the Trump administration’s Energy Department. Mumgaard also said there are offers on the table to build nuclear fission power plants outside the United States, underscoring his point that capital and projects can move to other jurisdictions. He framed U.S. fusion development as an energy security maneuver, arguing that fusion could remove geostrategic conflict over natural resources: “All the geostrategic strife we have right now is about power in the form of natural resources. Who has them? What are they? What boats are they on through what body of water? Fusion takes all of that off the table.”

For the energy sector, the implication is that fusion is shifting from a purely scientific challenge to an industrial and geopolitical one. A $10 billion federal commitment would signal that Washington treats fusion as critical infrastructure rather than a long-dated research program. Without it, private fusion developers may still proceed, but the timing and location of first commercial deployment could favor countries offering stronger public backing. The comparison to artificial intelligence suggests policymakers should expect fusion competition to intensify along similar lines: concentrated capital, national security framing, and a race for first-mover advantage in a technology that could power the next economy.

The analysis is limited by its source base. The dossier contains one full-text secondary report from Heatmap News, and the $10 billion figure is presented as an industry association request rather than enacted policy or a formal government proposal. The report does not provide independent verification of the offers Mumgaard referenced, the specific terms of Commonwealth Fusion’s recent $1 billion raise, or comparable Chinese fusion investment figures. What to watch next is whether the Fusion Industry Association’s request gains traction in Congress or the administration, whether Commonwealth Fusion or other developers announce siting decisions outside the United States, and whether any federal fusion funding package moves beyond bipartisan rhetorical support into appropriations.
