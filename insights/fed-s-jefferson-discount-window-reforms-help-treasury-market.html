Fed's Jefferson: discount window reforms help Treasury market

Federal Reserve Vice Chair Philip Jefferson said modernization of the discount window is not only bolstering bank liquidity but also shoring up Treasury market

Ben Rohr September 23, 2026 2 min read 1 source
Share LinkedIn X / Twitter

Federal Reserve Vice Chair Philip Jefferson used a Tuesday speech at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York's annual U.S. Treasury Market Conference to argue that reforms to the central bank's discount window are doing more than improving bank liquidity. They are also reinforcing the functioning of the U.S. Treasury market, which he described as "the foundation for pricing risk across all asset classes, facilitating the efficient allocation of capital throughout our economy." The remarks matter because the discount window is the Fed's lending facility of last resort, and its usability during stress can determine whether banks sell Treasury holdings into a falling market or borrow against them instead.

Jefferson outlined three categories of reforms implemented over the past three years: standardization of operations across the Federal Reserve System, technological updates, and greater collaboration with the Federal Home Loan Banks. All 12 reserve banks now operate under the same collateral framework with common loan valuation models and processing technologies, removing longstanding variability. The Fed also introduced Discount Window Direct in 2024, a digital platform that allows banks to pledge collateral online and chat with reserve bank officials. Jefferson noted that 60% of discount window borrowing now takes place this way, and the system accepts electronic signatures, simpler forms, faster enrollment, and automated pledged loan lists.

The Treasury market connection is direct. Treasury securities are the most readily used collateral for bank borrowing because they are government-backed and present no credit risk, so they are accepted at the discount window with a minimal haircut. Jefferson said this makes the discount window the optimal place for banks to turn with their Treasury holdings during periods of stress, as they did during the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic in March 2020, preventing an outright firesale that could have damaged the sovereign debt market. "For institutions that have arrangements in place, this means that pledging Treasury collateral and receiving a same-day loan are straightforward and efficient," he said.

One area of ongoing work is coordination with the Federal Home Loan Banks, which often provide day-to-day liquidity through a less scrupulous collateral underwriting process. A key issue during the 2023 banking stress was the inability of troubled institutions to move collateral from the FHLBs to the Fed. Jefferson said the central bank has sought to smooth out that process, noting that financial conditions and liquidity needs can shift quickly. "We and our FHLB partners believe our ongoing efforts and continued commitment to this coordination put us in a much better position than three years ago," he said.

The evidence base is limited to a single secondary account of Jefferson's speech, so the analysis cannot independently verify the operational details or quantify the impact on Treasury market liquidity. The source does not provide data on discount window borrowing volumes, haircut levels, or the frequency of collateral transfers from the FHLBs. What to watch is whether the Fed publishes further details on the FHLB coordination effort and whether future stress episodes show reduced hesitation among healthy banks to use the window, a friction Jefferson said the reforms are intended to reduce.
