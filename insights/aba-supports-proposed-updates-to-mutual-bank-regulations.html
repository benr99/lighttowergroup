ABA supports proposed updates to mutual bank regulations

The American Bankers Association says a Federal Reserve proposal to modernize mutual bank rules would make the framework more flexible and better suited to the

Ben Rohr October 05, 2026 2 min read 1 source

The American Bankers Association has endorsed a Federal Reserve effort to modernize the regulatory framework for mutual banks, arguing the proposed changes would make the framework more flexible and better suited to the distinctive structure and capital needs of mutual institutions. The Fed's proposal, described as the first comprehensive update of mutual bank regulations since 1993, is significant because mutual banks operate under a different ownership model than stock banks, and their access to regulatory capital has historically been constrained by rules written for shareholder-owned institutions.

The proposal would amend the Fed's capital rule to clarify that mutual capital instruments may qualify as regulatory capital, including as Tier 1 capital. It would also amend Regulation MM to eliminate certain dividend waiver requirements and remove the requirement that subsidiary holding companies of mutual holding companies obtain federal charters. In its letter to the Fed, the ABA said it strongly supports the proposed changes and believes they should serve as the basis for further reform. The association specifically highlighted the proposed treatment of mutual capital certificates as a useful template for all mutual bank regulators to develop clear, consistent, and predictable standards for special deposits and other instruments sharing the essential characteristics of mutual capital certificates.

The evidence comes from a single ABA Banking Journal article published on October 5, 2026, which summarizes the association's position and the Fed's proposal. The article is a secondary source reporting on the ABA's letter to the Federal Reserve. It does not include the full text of the Fed's proposal or the ABA's letter, and it does not provide independent analysis from regulators or other industry groups. The ABA's recommendations for further changes include creating a clear path to capital recognition for certificates conforming to the model terms, permitting disclosure of nonbinding target distribution rates, and further streamlining the proposed dividend-waiver framework.

For the mutual banking sector, the proposal could expand capital-raising options and reduce administrative burdens. Mutual institutions, which are owned by their depositors rather than shareholders, have fewer avenues to raise equity-like capital than stock banks. Clarifying that mutual capital instruments may qualify as Tier 1 capital could improve their ability to meet regulatory capital requirements and support lending. Eliminating dividend waiver requirements and the federal charter requirement for subsidiary holding companies would reduce compliance costs and operational complexity. The ABA's support suggests the industry views the proposal as a meaningful step, though the article does not quantify the number of institutions affected or the potential capital impact.

The analysis is limited by the single-source nature of the evidence. The dossier does not include the Fed's proposal text, the ABA's full letter, or any response from other regulators such as the FDIC or OCC. It also does not indicate whether the proposal applies to all mutual banks or only those supervised by the Federal Reserve, nor does it specify a comment deadline or expected timeline for finalization. The article does not report any opposition or concerns from consumer groups, credit unions, or other stakeholders. Future developments to watch include whether the Fed adopts the ABA's additional recommendations, how other mutual bank regulators respond to the template for mutual capital certificates, and whether the final rule preserves the Tier 1 capital treatment for mutual capital instruments.
