Nvidia cuts OpenAI's Ohio backstop to under $120bn

The smaller guarantee shifts credit risk back to OpenAI and leaves later phases without a named backstop.

Ben Rohr August 17, 2026 2 min read 1 source
Share LinkedIn X / Twitter

The number that matters is not 10 gigawatts; it is $250bn — now less than $120bn. Nvidia has cut the financial guarantee underpinning OpenAI’s proposed Ohio data centre project, according to the Wall Street Journal, limiting its backstop to the first phase of a campus that would be the largest data centre project announced to date if built in full.

The site, developed by SoftBank subsidiary SB Energy, is still reported as a 10-gigawatt campus. OpenAI is still negotiating a binding lease for the entire campus. The revised guarantee therefore covers a fraction of the eventual buildout, not the whole project. A deal on the first phase could be signed as early as this weekend, but the full lease and the backstops behind later phases remain open.

The revision is best read as a capital-constraint event, not a power or land problem. The 10GW headline masks the real question: whose balance sheet absorbs the gap between OpenAI’s lease promise and the cost of a phased buildout. OpenAI remains unprofitable despite an $852bn valuation, and its ability to fund commitments at this scale independently has not been tested. A large Nvidia guarantee was effectively substituting for OpenAI’s own credit; cutting it by more than half puts more of that burden back on OpenAI, or on whoever else is brought in to share it.

The driver, per the Journal’s sourcing, is investor pressure on Nvidia’s risk exposure from large financing commitments. The timing is important. The change lands days after Nvidia partnered with six major financial institutions to launch compute financing platforms aimed at raising over $500bn in third-party capital for AI infrastructure. Together, the two moves look like a reallocation of risk: Nvidia still wants to back the buildout, but with more exposure shifted to outside capital rather than carried on its own balance sheet.

That is the merchant GPU cloud pattern now moving upward. Nvidia’s backstop mechanism was designed for smaller merchant GPU cloud operators carrying a refinancing wall; CoreWeave’s disclosure of $35bn in total debt at the end of Q2 showed the size of that problem. The Ohio revision applies the same caution to a deal an order of magnitude larger. The circular-financing loop, in which every dollar of Nvidia investment can become several dollars of Nvidia chip purchases, is visibly strained and drawing regulatory and market scrutiny. Cutting the guarantee by more than half does not break the loop, but it limits how much of the downside Nvidia absorbs directly, even as the bank-backed platforms expand the loop itself.

The unresolved question is not whether the first phase moves forward, but who signs for the remaining phases and on what terms. If OpenAI cannot close a full 10GW binding lease without Nvidia’s full credit support, the campus’s later phases will need a new capital partner. The signal to monitor is not another headline about scale; it is the name on the next guarantee.
