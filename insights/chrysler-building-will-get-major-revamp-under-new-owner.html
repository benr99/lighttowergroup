Chrysler Building will get major revamp under new owner

Tishman Speyer plans to buy the Chrysler Building and invest $235 million in a restoration program, ending a period of uncertainty for the Art Deco landmark.

Ben Rohr October 08, 2026 3 min read 1 source
Share LinkedIn X / Twitter

After more than a year of uncertainty, the Chrysler Building has a new owner with major renovation plans. Tishman Speyer announced on Wednesday that it will buy the iconic Art Deco skyscraper and restore the office building inside and out, investing $235 million plus ground lease payments to The Cooper Union, which has owned the land beneath the tower since 1902. The transaction closes a tumultuous chapter for the landmark, which was put back on the market in May 2025 for the first time since 2019 after its previous owners fell behind on more than $21 million in rent owed to Cooper Union. The announcement matters because it signals a credible attempt to reposition a distressed trophy asset at a time when older office buildings face particular pressure.

The mechanics of the deal are shaped by the building's unusual ownership structure. The 1,046-foot-tall tower, designed by William Van Alen and completed in 1930, sits on land owned by Cooper Union under a 150-year ground lease. Tishman Speyer will pay rent to Cooper Union as part of that lease, which provides a permanent source of financial support for the institution. Tishman Speyer and its partners, including PSP Investments and other institutional partners, will use 100 percent equity to finance the ground lease and redevelopment plan. The renovation program includes restoring the famous facade and its distinctive crown, modernizing mechanical, elevator, electrical, and air-handling systems, and creating an amenities program on the 61st floor with an upscale lounge, outdoor access, food and beverage offerings, and gathering space. A second amenities suite in the underground arcade will feature fitness, wellness, and meeting spaces.

The evidence comes from a single full-text report by 6sqft, which draws on the announcement and prior reporting. The source notes that this is not Tishman Speyer's first involvement with the building: in 1997, the firm and partners purchased the building and several other properties for $220 million and later restored many Art Deco fixtures, including an Edward Trumbull mural on the lobby ceiling. During the Great Recession in 2008, Tishman Speyer sold much of its investment to an Abu Dhabi investment fund for $800 million. In 2019, Signa and RFR bought the property for $151 million, a discounted price reflecting the expensive ground lease of $32 million a year and the tower's deteriorated condition. The report also details the recent legal conflict: after Signa filed for insolvency in 2023, RFR's Aby Rosen attempted to renegotiate lease terms, but no deal was reached. Cooper Union alleged that RFR stopped paying rent in June 2024, leading to lease termination and eviction in early 2025. RFR later sued Cooper Union, arguing that the college's handling of Israel-Palestine protests unsettled the real estate community and drove away tenants; a judge dismissed the case and awarded Cooper Union damages for unpaid rent.

For the commercial real estate sector, the plan offers a case study in repositioning an older landmark through prebuilt space and amenity investment. Tishman Speyer said it will prebuild suites for 75 percent of the building's current and future vacancy, an approach the firm applied at the adjacent 6 Grand Central Tower to allow tenants to move in quickly. The firm aims to reposition the 1.3 million-square-foot tower as Manhattan's "most distinctive new boutique office building." The emphasis on hospitality, wellness, and turnkey suites reflects a broader response to post-pandemic office demand, though the dossier does not provide independent market data on vacancy or rents. The use of 100 percent equity also suggests a deliberate avoidance of debt financing, but the source does not state the total purchase price or the new ground lease terms beyond the investment amount.

Several limitations remain. The dossier is based on one secondary source, and key figures such as the acquisition price, the new ground lease rent, and the expected timeline for completion are not specified. The report does not include tenant response, leasing commitments, or independent analysis of whether the $235 million investment will be sufficient to address the building's reported problems, which included rodent infestations, cracked ceilings, broken elevators, and dirty drinking water under prior ownership. What to watch is whether Tishman Speyer can convert the renovation plan into signed leases and whether the repositioning strategy, tested at 6 Grand Central Tower, performs similarly at a landmark with higher operating costs and a ground lease that has already contributed to two distressed ownership periods.
