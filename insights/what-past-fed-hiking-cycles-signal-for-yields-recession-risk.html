What Past Fed Hiking Cycles Signal for Yields, Recession Risk

The Federal Reserve's quarter-point increase to a 3.75% to 4% target range began its first tightening campaign since July 2023, with policymakers signaling that at least one more increase may be needed before year-end. Past hiking cycles have ranged from modest adjustments to the aggressive inflation-fighting campaigns of the 1970s and early 1980s. Larger and faster cycles were more likely to precede recessions, although a recession was not inevitable.

Historical data shows the most aggressive tightening occurred when inflation was already deeply embedded. From March 1972 through July 1974, the Fed raised its policy rate by roughly 930 basis points as CPI was running near 3.6% at the start. The campaign overlapped with a recession that began in November 1973 and lasted 16 months. The Fed's next major campaign began in January 1977, when CPI was about 5.2%, with rates rising roughly 1,300 basis points through April 1980. The central bank resumed aggressive tightening in August 1980, with CPI near 12.9%, raising rates another 1,000 basis points through June 1981.

The 1994-95 cycle offers a contrasting lesson. The Fed raised rates by 300 basis points in 13 months after beginning with CPI around 2.5%. The 10-year Treasury yield rose about 200 basis points during the 1994 bond selloff, yet the economy avoided a recession. This showed that higher long-term yields can tighten financial conditions on their own, potentially doing some of the Fed's work. The most recent prior hiking cycle, from March 2022 through July 2023, was historically rapid, lifting rates 525 basis points in 16 months after CPI reached about 7.9%.

What remains unknown is whether the current cycle will follow the 1994 pattern of a bond-market repricing without recession or the 1988-89 pattern where a recession began about 14 months after the final hike. The level of inflation, the pace of rate hikes, and the Treasury market's response have been critical in determining outcomes, but history offers no single answer.
