Limited-Service Hotel Loans Lead CMBS Lodging in Nonperforming Rate and Refinancing Risk

Limited-service hotels account for just 12.6% of the securitized lodging book but 21.8% of its nonperforming balance, with a 10.34% nonperforming rate that far

Ben Rohr September 29, 2026 2 min read 1 source
Share LinkedIn X / Twitter

Loans secured by limited-service hotels are the most likely part of the securitized lodging market to be nonperforming, according to Trepp data. Limited service accounts for $11.58 billion, or 12.6%, of the $92.18 billion securitized lodging book, but it represents $1.20 billion, or 21.8%, of the nonperforming balance. Its nonperforming rate of 10.34% compares with 5.86% for full service and 3.89% for extended stay. That concentration of distress matters because it signals where refinancing pressure will be most acute as loans approach maturity.

The segment has also lost the most coverage since securitization. Limited-service's balance-weighted median debt service coverage ratio fell from 1.81x at underwriting to 1.37x, and its median net cash flow is 9.7% below underwriting. Extended-stay has held occupancy and coverage closest to underwriting, although its median net cash flow is 8.1% lower. Full-service, which includes four large Las Vegas casino-resort loans, sits between the two on coverage. This deterioration in cash flow relative to debt service reduces the segment's ability to absorb higher financing costs or principal paydowns.

Debt yield, measured as net cash flow divided by loan balance, shows where refinancing will be hardest. Many lenders use 8.0% as a refinancing threshold, and balance below that level would generally need a principal paydown, additional equity, or different loan terms to refinance in full. Limited-service carries the largest share of balance below that threshold at 19.9%, against 16.4% for the sector. The maturity picture compounds the problem: limited-service has the least capacity of the three segments to push maturities past 2028. Its balance reaching a hard maturity by 2028, $5.43 billion, is larger than its $5.16 billion of stated maturities through 2028. Some limited-service loans are already past their stated maturity and are running on extensions that end inside the 2026 to 2028 window, so they reach a hard date without appearing among stated maturities. Full-service and extended-stay carry more remaining extension capacity, with stated maturities through 2028 at 2.51 times and 1.83 times their hard maturities, respectively.

The $5.43 billion of limited-service balance reaching a hard maturity by 2028 carries a balance-weighted median debt yield of 9.76%. Still, $1.69 billion of it, or 31.1%, is below 8.0% and would generally need a paydown, additional equity, or different terms to refinance in full. Full-service still holds most of the nonperforming dollars, 68.5%, because it is more than five times the size of limited service. But limited-service is where a dollar of balance is most likely to be nonperforming. The implication for CMBS investors and servicers is that limited-service should be surveilled by rate and refinancing capacity, while full-service should be watched by the individual large loans behind its dollar total.

The analysis is based on a single source read in full, and the underlying data are balance-weighted medians and segment aggregates rather than loan-level disclosures. The evidence does not identify specific properties, sponsors, or loan terms, and it does not explain why limited-service performance has diverged from extended-stay or full-service. What to watch next is whether the 31.1% of limited-service hard-maturity balance below the 8.0% debt yield threshold can access paydowns or alternative financing, and whether the extension capacity of full-service and extended-stay loans masks similar stress beyond 2028.
