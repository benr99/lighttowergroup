As feds retreat, California boosts fair-lending enforcement

California's legislature passed a bill empowering state regulators to examine mortgage lenders for fair-lending compliance as federal enforcement retreats.

Ben Rohr September 03, 2026 1 min read 2 sources
Share LinkedIn X / Twitter

On Aug. 27, the California State Legislature passed the California Fair Lending Examination Act, a bill that would require the state's Department of Financial Protection and Innovation to examine mortgage lenders for discriminatory actions under both state and federal law. The measure, authored by Democratic Assemblymember Mia Bonta, now awaits Gov. Gavin Newsom's signature. It arrives as the Trump administration has scaled back enforcement of antidiscrimination rules in banking, including rules against unintentional discrimination.

Proponents argue the bill is a necessary response to that federal retreat. Gregory Squires, an emeritus professor of public policy and sociology at George Washington University, said the federal government "is no longer in the fair-housing business" and that state action is needed because "it's clear that the federal government isn't going to do anything." The National Community Reinvestment Coalition also welcomed the bill, with policy director Tara Flynn calling it "an important first step, but there is more to do."

The legislation could push lenders to pay closer attention to fair-lending and fair-housing laws even if its effect is partly psychological, Squires said. However, some industry observers are skeptical it will change much. Chris Willis, a partner at Troutman Pepper Locke, noted that lenders already anticipate enforcement could return under a future Democratic administration, and that the U.S. Supreme Court has upheld disparate impact liability under the Fair Housing Act, limiting how far federal agencies can roll back the doctrine.

What remains unknown is whether Newsom will sign the bill and how aggressively California regulators would enforce it in practice. The dossier does not establish the bill's effective date, examination frequency, or penalties for noncompliance. It also does not quantify how many lenders would be subject to the new examinations or whether other states might follow California's lead.
