Student Housing’s Hidden Credit Risk: Low Defaults Mask $1.25 Billion in Sub-1.0x DSCR Loans

A 1.4% non-performing rate suggests limited distress in securitized student housing, but $1.25 billion in current loans with sub-1.0x DSCRs points to unrealized

Ben Rohr September 23, 2026 3 min read 1 source
Share LinkedIn X / Twitter

The securitized student housing market presents a deceptively calm surface. A balance-weighted non-performing rate of just 1.4% suggests limited realized distress across the sector. Yet that headline figure masks a meaningful pocket of potential payment risk: $1.25 billion of outstanding student housing balance remains current on payments despite reporting debt service coverage ratios below 1.00x. A DSCR below 1.00x indicates that reported property cash flow is insufficient to cover debt service. These loans may require borrower support or, in some cases, have built-in interest reserves to service required payments. The divergence between low defaults and weak coverage matters because it means current performance is not, on its own, a reliable signal of underlying credit health.

The mechanics of this hidden risk become clearer when the $29.26 billion securitized student housing lending market is broken down by capital source. Agency lenders—Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, and Ginnie Mae—make up most balances and generally lend to stabilized buildings at low leverage. Agency student housing loans are well-covered overall, with a 1.74x median DSCR on a net cash flow basis. Only 3.9% of the agency balance has a sub-breakeven DSCR, and the agency non-performing rate is just 0.5%. Their median debt yield, calculated as net cash flow divided by loan balance, is 12.2%, compared with 8.1% for private-label CMBS. Non-agency lenders, including private-label CMBS and CRE CLOs, are underwritten with higher leverage or for transitional business plans, and the differences become apparent when comparing DSCR and non-performing rates.

The evidence points to very different risk profiles across non-agency capital sources. Private-label CMBS accounts for 18.4% of student housing balance and 56.8% of all non-performing balance, the largest share of realized distress of the three capital sources. The weak loans have largely already defaulted: private-label CMBS carries $236.95 million below a 1.00x DSCR against $230.18 million already non-performing. A 4.3% non-performing rate is high, but it is not a leading indicator of more defaults. CRE CLOs present the opposite pattern. They commonly finance transitional properties, where interest reserves can help fund debt service while a business plan is executed. In the dataset, 20 of the 76 properties financed by CRE CLOs have sub-breakeven DSCRs, yet 97.3% of CRE CLO balance remains current on payments. The borrower's ability to execute the transitional business plan is what will drive refinancing prospects.

The sector-level implication is that potential risk is concentrated in the smallest securitized lender category. Agency loans are performing and well-covered. Private-label CMBS has elevated non-performing balance but not much unrealized weakness behind it. CRE CLOs are the opposite: they have the largest proportion of sub-1.0 DSCR loans, short-term floating-rate terms, and dependence on successfully repositioning the property. While CRE CLOs are the smallest of the securitized lenders, they carry the majority of potential risk. For investors and analysts tracking student housing credit, the low aggregate non-performing rate should not be read as an all-clear signal; the $1.25 billion in current but sub-breakeven loans represents a contingent liability that could surface if transitional business plans stall or refinancing conditions tighten.

The analysis is bounded by a single source read in full, and several limitations apply. The dossier does not provide loan-level detail on the specific properties behind the $1.25 billion in sub-1.0x DSCR loans, nor does it disclose the size or duration of any interest reserves supporting CRE CLO payments. It also does not break out maturity schedules, sponsor quality, or property-level occupancy trends that would help assess the likelihood of successful repositioning. What to watch next is whether the 20 CRE CLO properties with sub-breakeven DSCRs can improve cash flow before their short-term floating-rate debt matures, and whether the small gap between private-label CMBS sub-1.0x DSCR balance and already non-performing balance widens under further stress.
