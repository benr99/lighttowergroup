Lenders begin pricing local opposition into data centre credit risk

US financing leans toward states seen as welcoming as $130bn in projects stall and moratoriums spread.

Ben Rohr August 15, 2026 2 min read 1 source
Share LinkedIn X / Twitter

US data centre projects worth $130 billion were delayed or cancelled in the first quarter of 2026 alone, according to Data Center Watch. The binding constraint in these cases is political permission, and lenders have begun to treat it as a credit input rather than a local nuisance.

Senior bankers told Reuters they now weigh community sentiment alongside project readiness and credit quality when assessing loans, and are leaning toward developments in states seen as more welcoming to data centres. The public withdrawals that preceded the shift are specific: Amazon Web Services pulled its application for a 2.5 million square foot project near the Calvert Cliffs Nuclear Power Plant in Calvert County, Maryland, at a time when a petition against it had collected more than 10,000 signatures. AWS and the county have not confirmed that opposition caused the withdrawal, but county officials are preparing to consider a six-month moratorium.

That withdrawal followed QTS's July decision to pull its $30 billion Digital Gateway project in Prince William County, Virginia. AWS had already exited a Louisa County, Virginia project in 2025—one earlier and larger than the Calvert County proposal—after residents raised concerns over water use and land impact. Data Center Watch counted at least 75 major projects delayed or cancelled in the same quarter, with organised local opposition cited as a contributing factor.

What makes permission risk material to lenders is scale. Moody's Ratings says North America absorbed a record 25GW of capacity in the first half of 2026, straining electricity and water infrastructure enough to raise credit risk for the state and local governments hosting projects. At least 14 states are considering statewide moratoriums in 2026. That is a regulatory timeline risk: a project can hold a signed lease, an interconnection agreement, and building permits, and still face a new moratorium before first energization.

For lenders, the reasonable inference is that community opposition behaves like an interconnection queue delay. It extends the period during which capital is committed but debt service has begun and project cash flow has not. The source material does not name the banks or asset managers adjusting terms, and it does not disclose the exact pricing change. What is reported is the direction of underwriting: loan allocation is tilting toward states viewed as more welcoming.

Such a tilt suggests a two-tier capital map. Michigan and West Virginia have introduced incentives to attract data centre investment even as other states pull back. Lenders leaning toward welcoming states are not avoiding risk; they are trading one approval risk for a different infrastructure risk. Moody's warning adds a second layer: if a state or county has borrowed against expected data centre tax revenue, a moratorium or cancellation becomes a public finance shock, not just a project-level problem.

Still unresolved is whether community opposition becomes a standard underwriting covenant or remains a qualitative screen. The next marker is not another hyperscaler campus announcement; it is whether Calvert County adopts its six-month moratorium and whether lenders begin to price the difference between states that invite data centres and states that pause them.
