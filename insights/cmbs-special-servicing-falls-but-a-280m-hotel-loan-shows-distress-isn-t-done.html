CMBS Special Servicing Falls, But a $280M Hotel Loan Shows Distress Isn't Done

Office and lodging drove the decline, while new retail and multifamily defaults signal a shifting maturity wall.

Ben Rohr August 11, 2026 2 min read 1 source
Share LinkedIn X / Twitter

One chart, one argument

The CMBS special servicing rate fell 11 basis points in July to 11.09%. That is the good news. The bad news arrived with it: a $280 million loan backed by two full-service beachfront hotels in Santa Monica, California, moved into special servicing because its balloon payment is coming due and refinancing looks unlikely.

The improvement was led by office, where the rate dropped 53 basis points to 16.58%. Lodging eased 26 basis points to 8.63%, and industrial slipped 3 basis points to 1.34%. After months of increases, those drops suggest some distressed loans are finally being resolved—modified, sold, or foreclosed.

But elsewhere the numbers pointed the other way. Retail’s special servicing rate jumped 33 basis points to 13.28%, the result of a wave of regional-mall loans transferring to special servicing for maturity default. Multifamily added 16 basis points to 8.39%, while mixed-use crept up 2 basis points to 11.93%.

The divide reveals a distress migration. Office and lodging, which absorbed the earliest blows of the rate-hiking cycle, now appear to be working through their backlogs. Retail and multifamily, whose borrowers face steeper refinancing hurdles after the rate surge, are just beginning to hit the maturity wall. The $280 million hotel loan illustrates the point: a well-located asset still could not escape a trip to the special servicer because the debt cannot be refinanced at acceptable terms.

What the data doesn't show is whether office loans exiting special servicing did so at a profit for lenders or a wipeout for borrowers. The resolution likely involved principal write-downs, debt-for-equity swaps, or distressed sales. Whatever the mechanics, the shrinking pool of office loans in special servicing suggests servicers are finding ways to close out troubled loans, even if the recoveries are painful.

For retail, the story is only beginning. Regional malls have struggled with anchor closings and low valuations for years. Now, with maturities arriving, many borrowers cannot replace expiring CMBS debt. The result is a transfer to special servicing, where the servicer will decide whether to extend, restructure, or move toward foreclosure. Each decision tests whether current property values can support the old loan amounts.

The overall rate, at 11.09%, remains historically high. One month's decline does not mean the distress cycle is over. New transfers totaled $1.69 billion across 41 loans in July alone. The pipeline of upcoming maturities, particularly in retail and multifamily, could push the rate higher again before year-end. Market participants should watch whether the office-led improvement holds when the next round of retail and multifamily loans hits the special servicer's desk. The Santa Monica hotel loan will be an early test: a trophy asset that ran out of time.
