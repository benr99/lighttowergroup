OCVibe’s $5B Bet Rests on a Landlord-Paid Kitchen

The Samuelis are using patient capital to test whether an arena district can draw weekday demand before apartments arrive.

Ben Rohr August 19, 2026 2 min read 1 source
Share LinkedIn X / Twitter

One chart, one argument

The landlord is paying for the kitchen equipment at Katella Commons, the 50,000-square-foot food hall opening in Anaheim in 2027. Tenants, Scott Frick told Commercial Observer, “basically bring in your own knives and we’ll turn on the menu boards.” That arrangement explains the Samueli family’s $5 billion bet on OCVibe better than the renderings do. The problem is not filling 100 acres on event nights. It is staying alive when the Honda Center goes dark.

The project is the 100-acre master-planned district taking shape around the Ducks’ arena. Frick, senior vice president of real estate and district operations for Orange County Sports & Entertainment, says the land has been assembled from almost 10 parcels over eight years. The plan includes 170,000 square feet of mass-timber office, more than 2,000 apartments, two hotels, more than 35 dining concepts, and 20 acres of parks and plazas. The first major pieces open in 2027: Katella Commons, a 50,000-square-foot market hall; a 5,000-capacity concert venue; and The Weave, where flexible-office operator Kiln has committed to a full floor.

The decision that matters is not the mix. It is the sequence. Most developers, Frick said, would deliver apartments first to start cash flow and add placemaking later. The Samuelis reversed that order. They replaced surface parking with structured decks, kept the arena operating through a Ducks playoff run, and are opening parks, food halls, and the concert venue before the first apartments. “The family really wanted to invest in the placemaking first,” Frick said.

That could look like an expensive luxury. The project has no outside investors, which means no joint-venture partner demanding early returns. But the real mechanism is subtler. Because the family owns the team, the arena, the parking decks, and the surrounding real estate, Frick can hand restaurant tenants the operating calendar. “We’ve got staff arriving at 4. We’ve got guests that’ll start arriving at 5:30. Puck drops at 7,” he said. The pitch to tenants is not “trust the district.” It is “here is your second table turn.”

Honda Center draws about 2 million attendees a year. The 2028 Olympics will push roughly half a million people through the arena in 11 days, by Frick’s estimate. That is a surge, not a base case. The new residential and office uses are intended to provide the Monday-through-Sunday population that event traffic cannot. Frick pointed to Orange County supply constraints: residential inventory growth has slowed to less than 1 percent a year, rents are high, and office construction is dry. The family originally entitled more than a million square feet of office; after COVID, it reworked the entitlement and kept only 170,000 square feet. That cut is the clearest evidence that OCVibe is not a pre-2020 sprawl play.

The remaining question is whether the restaurant subsidy works. Landlord-funded kitchen fit-outs lower the cost of entry for operators such as Roy Choi’s Best Friend, the first announced full-service restaurant. But lower upfront capital does not create weekday demand. The project’s first true test will be a Tuesday in February with no Ducks game and no concert, when Katella Commons has to fill seats on its own economics. If it does, the placemaking-first sequence will look like a structural advantage. If it doesn’t, the Samuelis will have bought the kitchens and still been left waiting for tenants’ second shifts.
