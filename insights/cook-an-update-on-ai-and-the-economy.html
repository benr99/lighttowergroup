Cook, An Update on AI and the Economy

Federal Reserve Governor Lisa D. Cook framed AI as a general-purpose technology and outlined how AI-driven investment is creating both sector-specific and econo

Ben Rohr September 28, 2026 3 min read 1 source
Share LinkedIn X / Twitter

Federal Reserve Governor Lisa D. Cook used her September 28, 2026 keynote at Oakland Tech Week to update her thinking on artificial intelligence and the economy, and the speech matters because it shows a sitting monetary policymaker distinguishing between inflation pressures that should resolve on their own and those that could require a policy response. Cook described AI as "the most significant technological shift of our lifetime" and a general-purpose technology on par with or exceeding the steam engine, electricity, and the internet. That framing is consequential for the Federal Reserve's dual mandate because it implies AI's effects will spread broadly across sectors, labor markets, and prices over time rather than remaining confined to a narrow tech boom.

The material mechanics of her argument center on how AI investment is affecting inflation. Cook noted that inflation has remained above the Fed's 2 percent target over the past five years, and that over the past year one contributing factor has been AI-driven investment. Prices for AI-related goods such as chips, computers, and software have surged. She attributed some of those steep price increases to a shift in demand toward AI-related sectors rather than an increase in economy-wide demand. When demand is concentrated in one sector, she explained, goods and services in that sector can be pushed onto a steep part of the supply curve. Had that demand been spread evenly across the economy, the overall price index would not climb as much. Cook said this kind of pressure should resolve as supply chains adjust and efficiency gains accrue, without policy intervention.

The evidence she offered also points to broader, economy-wide pressure from AI-fueled demand. Cook said data-center investment relies on inputs such as construction labor and energy that are broadly used across many sectors, meaning increased AI investment could introduce price pressure to those other sectors. The dossier includes specific figures from the speech: companies have only spent a small fraction of the $2 trillion in announced plans, electricity and water costs are each up around 5 percent over the past year, and some prices are running over a 3 percent annual pace so far this year compared with before the pandemic. Cook was careful to frame these as potential attributions rather than definitive causal findings, and she warned that attempting to fight sector-specific inflation with monetary policy could be a mistake because the Fed's tools are too blunt to target narrow sectors and addressing relative price shifts is not the Fed's role.

For markets and the broader economy, the speech suggests the Fed is monitoring AI investment as a source of near-term price pressure while remaining reluctant to tighten policy in response to sector-specific demand shifts. That stance could matter for investors in AI-related hardware, data centers, energy, and construction, because it implies the Fed is unlikely to treat surging chip or software prices alone as a reason to raise rates. At the same time, Cook's emphasis on economy-wide inputs such as construction labor and energy means sustained AI investment could feed into broader inflation measures that the Fed does care about. Financial institutions also face a supervisory signal: Cook said the Fed has encouraged and continues to encourage responsible use of AI within the financial sector consistent with safe and sound practices and applicable law, while leaving any broader government role in AI's trajectory to elected officials.

The analysis is bounded by a single primary source read in full, so the speech should be treated as one policymaker's framework rather than a consensus Federal Reserve position. The dossier does not include corroborating data on AI-related price increases, the $2 trillion in announced plans, or the specific inflation figures Cook cited. It also does not provide the full text of her remarks on maximum employment, financial stability, or operational preparedness, which she said AI affects. What to watch is whether future Fed communications quantify AI-driven inflation more precisely, whether data-center investment broadens into economy-wide price pressure, and whether elected officials take up the government role that Cook explicitly left to them.
