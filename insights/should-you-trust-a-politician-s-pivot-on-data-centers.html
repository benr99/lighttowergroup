Should You Trust a Politician’s Pivot on Data Centers?

Texas Governor Greg Abbott celebrated a $40 billion Google data center investment in November 2025, then called for prohibiting AI data centers in rural neighbo

Ben Rohr September 29, 2026 3 min read 1 source
Share LinkedIn X / Twitter

In November 2025, Texas Governor Greg Abbott announced a $40 billion Google investment in his state and declared, “Texas is the epicenter of AI development, where companies can pair innovation with expanding energy.” Seven months later, at a campaign stop in East Texas, he delivered a sharply different message: “We must prohibit them from building AI data centers in rural Texas neighborhoods.” Last week, Abbott instructed Texas’ environmental agency to stop issuing permits to data center projects until the state’s grid operator completes an audit of all data centers in the interconnection process. The pivot matters because it shows how quickly the political consensus around data center development is shifting, and it raises a practical question for developers, investors, and communities: which version of a politician’s position should they trust?

The material facts are narrow but concrete. The only dollar figure in the record is the $40 billion Google investment Abbott announced in November 2025. The permitting freeze is not a legislative ban; it is an instruction to the Texas environmental agency to halt permits until the grid operator finishes an audit of data centers in the interconnection queue. The dossier does not specify the audit’s timeline, scope, or what happens after it concludes. Abbott is not alone. On September 23, Maryland Governor Wes Moore, a Democrat, signed an executive order tying state incentives for large projects to a new review process, pledged that “the state will not go around a local community’s ‘no,’” and said he would ask lawmakers to repeal the state’s data center tax exemption passed in 2020. The same day, Kansas Democratic nominee Cindy Holscher, who voted for data center tax incentives as a state senator and now backs a moratorium, said she “certainly would vote differently based on the information we have now.” Teri Ann Hourihan, Arizona’s No Labels candidate, promised a “Day 1” moratorium on new data centers.

The evidence comes from a single secondary source, Heatmap News, read in full. The source reports tracking by The Impact Project, which counted 143 candidates for governor in 36 states and three territories. Of the 78 major party candidates, 69 have voiced positions on data centers. Thirty-eight candidates have staked out restrictive positions, while 31 are supportive, ranging from unequivocal support to reluctant support with significant safeguards. The Impact Project counted a candidate as supportive if they champion data center development, even if they want a pause or moratorium to take a closer look first. At least 30 candidates support a pause, halt, moratorium, or ban, including 20 Democrats and 10 Republicans. In five states—Maine, New Hampshire, Ohio, Oregon, and Texas—the Democratic and Republican candidates both clearly back a pause. Among sitting governors up for reelection, a quarter back a pause; among major party candidates newly seeking the job, 43% do. The 65 third-party and independent candidates lean further toward restriction: positions were documented for 32 of them, including 19 who back a pause, moratorium, or ban.

The sector implication is that data center development is becoming an election-year liability even in states that have aggressively courted it. The source reports that public opinion has shifted rapidly. In September 2025, Americans were evenly divided over whether they would support a data center being built near their homes. By August, 75% opposed one. In Virginia, the share of voters comfortable with a new data center in their community fell from 69% in 2023 to 35% in 2026. In May of this year, seven in 10 Americans expressed opposition, according to the source. For developers, the practical risk is not just a single permitting freeze but a widening set of state-level review processes, moratorium proposals, and incentive repeals that could change project economics and timelines. The fact that candidates in both parties are converging on pauses in five states suggests the restrictive position is becoming politically safe, which may encourage more candidates to adopt it.

The main limitation is that this is a single-source account, and the dossier does not include primary documents such as Abbott’s full instruction, the Texas grid operator’s audit parameters, or the text of Moore’s executive order. It also does not specify whether the $40 billion Google investment is committed capital, a multi-year plan, or an announcement subject to change. The source does not provide data on how many data center projects are currently in Texas’ interconnection queue or how many permits are affected by the freeze. What to watch is whether the Texas audit produces a defined endpoint or becomes an open-ended moratorium, whether other governors follow with formal executive actions rather than campaign statements, and whether the shift in public opinion continues to harden into binding policy after the election season ends.
