Mesa Approves $3B Legacy Park Under Strict Desert Water Rules

Ben Rohr September 20, 2026 3 min read 1 source
Share LinkedIn X / Twitter

The Mesa City Council approved Legacy Park and Gateway Crossing on Sept. 14, a roughly $3 billion mixed-use development positioned to support a growing corridor that includes Mesa Gateway Airport, Arizona State University's Polytechnic campus, Arizona Athletic Grounds and State Route 24. Mayor Mark Freeman called the project "a generational opportunity for Mesa and the largest private investment in our city's history to date," adding that it "can shape Mesa's economy for decades to come." The approval matters because it commits the city to a long-term public-private reimbursement structure while imposing unusually strict desert water compliance requirements on the developer, Vestar.

The project is planned as connected mixed-use destinations with hospitality, dining, retail, office and residential development, along with a public park, pedestrian connections, and transportation and utility improvements. Phase 1 is a $1-billion infrastructure and public park build targeting an October 2029 opening. A central feature is a 10-acre man-made lake that cannot use municipal tap water. To fill it, Vestar must buy private Type II groundwater rights, which under Arizona law are transferable water tickets not tied to a specific piece of land. Engineers must build a completely separate pipe system for the lake so it never accidentally connects to Mesa's clean drinking water, and the team must use special plastic liners and engineering methods to limit evaporation in the desert climate.

The evidence comes from a single Engineering News-Record article read in full, which quotes Arizona Department of Water Resources spokesperson Doug MacEachern on two regulatory constraints. First, non-exempt wells pumping more than 35 gallons per minute must submit a well impact analysis to ensure withdrawals do not cause "unreasonably increasing damage to surrounding land or other water users." Second, Arizona law generally prohibits using water to fill or refill a scenic or recreational body of water, with specific exceptions. The project qualifies under an exemption for bodies of water "located in a recreational facility that is open to the public and owned or operated by the United States, this state, a city, town or county," because the lake sits inside a master-planned park destined for public ownership.

The economic and market implications are substantial but contingent. A recent analysis cited in the article estimates the development could generate approximately $58.8 billion in economic activity, 13,500 jobs and $1.4 billion in tax revenue over 30 years. Under the agreement, Vestar must pay for all initial infrastructure upfront, and the city will gradually repay the developer through a rolling tax-sharing plan using sales and lodging taxes collected on-site, with total reimbursements capped at $76.4 million. The city will not release full funds until Vestar hits milestones, secures the water rights, and hands over the completed 20-acre park by the final December 31, 2034 deadline. This structure shifts early-stage capital risk to the developer while tying public money to verifiable performance and water compliance.

The main limitation is that the dossier contains one source read in full, so independent corroboration of the $58.8 billion economic impact estimate, the 13,500 jobs figure, and the $1.4 billion tax revenue projection is not available. The article also notes that a general contractor has not yet been selected for the infrastructure work, leaving construction timing and cost execution uncertain. What to watch next includes whether Vestar successfully acquires the private Type II groundwater rights, whether the state well impact analysis imposes additional conditions, and whether the October 2029 Phase 1 opening target holds as contractor selection and desert water engineering proceed.
