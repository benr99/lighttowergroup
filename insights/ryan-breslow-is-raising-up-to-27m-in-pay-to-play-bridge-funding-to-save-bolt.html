Ryan Breslow is raising up to $27M in pay-to-play bridge funding to save Bolt

The controversial founder of the checkout startup once valued at $11 billion is putting in $5 million of his own money.

Ben Rohr August 31, 2026 3 min read 1 source
Share LinkedIn X / Twitter

Ryan Breslow, the controversial entrepreneur who returned as CEO of Bolt last year, is attempting to keep the checkout processing startup alive with a bridge round of up to $27 million. The company, which hit an $11 billion valuation in early 2022 before plummeting 97% to $300 million, is raising the capital from existing investors as a convertible note. The financing includes a punitive pay-to-play provision, meaning backers who decline to participate will lose a large portion of their equity. Breslow is personally committing $5 million to the round, framing the move as a demonstration of his belief in the company. The stakes are high: although Breslow did not frame it as such, the new financing may be Bolt's last shot at survival.

The mechanics of the round are notable for their pressure on existing backers. The capital is structured as a convertible note, which will turn into equity at a discount once Bolt closes a future funding round. The pay-to-play provision adds a coercive element: investors who do not join will see their equity diluted or forfeited. Breslow estimates that participation from Bolt's roughly 100 investors will total at least $15 million, though he noted not everyone is expected to join in. The company's press release said the financing would allow Bolt to "capitalize on our recent operational milestones, clear legacy obligations, and ensure a seamless transition as we progress toward the closing of our full Series E2 round." Breslow did not respond to requests for comment on what constitutes those legacy obligations.

The evidence for this story comes from a single full-text source: a TechCrunch article published on August 31, 2026, based on interviews with Breslow and a company press release. The dossier contains no corroborating documents, financial statements, or independent investor confirmations beyond one unnamed angel backer who, through a wealth manager, confirmed plans to participate. The source is a tier-2 secondary outlet, meaning the reporting is credible but not independently verified by primary filings. Key figures—the $11 billion peak valuation, the 97% decline to $300 million, the $27 million bridge target, Breslow's $5 million personal commitment, and the estimated $15 million from existing investors—all come directly from Breslow's statements to TechCrunch. The failed 2022 round of $450 million at a $14 billion valuation is also cited, along with the lawsuit from BlackRock and Hedosophia that was later voluntarily dismissed.

The financing carries broader implications for the private equity and venture capital landscape. Bridge rounds with pay-to-play provisions are typically used when a company is either performing well and needs six to 12 months to hit its next milestone, or when it is running low on cash and needs time to restructure or reach profitability. Breslow declined to disclose how much cash Bolt has left, though he claimed the company is nearing profitability and returning to growth after years of shrinking revenue. The company has shrunk its headcount from 900 in 2021 to about 60 today, with Breslow arguing that AI is allowing the company to operate far more efficiently. He remains convinced he can restore Bolt to its former glory, telling TechCrunch, "I think we can be the Lyft to Stripe's Uber." The round also tests whether investors burned by the 2022 collapse will double down on a founder who has clashed with them for years.

Several limitations and unknowns remain. The dossier does not include Bolt's current cash position, revenue figures, or profitability metrics, making it impossible to independently assess whether the company is truly nearing profitability or simply running out of runway. The exact terms of the convertible note—including the discount rate, valuation cap, and the specific equity penalty for non-participating investors—are not disclosed. Breslow's claim that Bolt's board and a "majority of preferred" shareholders have signed off on the new fundraise is not independently verified. What to watch next: whether Bolt closes the full $27 million, how many of the roughly 100 existing investors actually participate, and whether the company can complete its Series E2 round. The outcome will signal whether Breslow's pay-to-play gambit can overcome the legacy of the collapsed $450 million round and the investor lawsuits that followed.
