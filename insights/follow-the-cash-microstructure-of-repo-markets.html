Follow the Cash! Microstructure of Repo Markets

The U.S. repo market is not a single venue but a mosaic of segments with distinct participants and settlement practices. A September 29, 2026, post from the New York Fed's Liberty Street Economics follows the cash through these segments to explain why large cash lenders often settle through third-party agents, why the interdealer market clears through a central counterparty, and why levered investors favor bilateral trades. The stakes are substantial: recent estimates put the size of U.S. repo markets above $13 trillion, dwarfing the roughly $200 billion daily total volume across federal funds, Eurodollar, and selected deposit markets. Understanding the microstructure is therefore essential for assessing short-term funding stability and the transmission of monetary policy.

The defining feature of a repo is collateral. One party sells a security, frequently a U.S. Treasury, for cash and promises to repurchase it later, usually the next business day. Collateral expands the motives for entering repo transactions and attracts a broader set of participants. Hedge funds may use repos to secure specific securities, while money market funds view the collateral as a way to reduce investment risk. This richness of counterparties and motives incentivizes customization in execution, clearing, and settlement. Participants evaluate not only rate, maturity, collateral type, and haircut, but also the operational, legal, and counterparty risks embedded in trade verification and final transfer of ownership.

Dealers sit at the center of this market. They intermediate between ultimate borrowers and lenders, earning a spread for taking on counterparty, collateral, and, to a lesser extent, maturity transformation risk. The New York Fed analysis cites estimates that in late 2024 dealers borrowed $3.7 trillion and lent $3.4 trillion per day. In the interdealer market, most Treasury repo trades clear through the Fixed Income Clearing Corporation, which becomes the counterparty to every trade. Central clearing offers netting benefits that reduce balance sheet costs and expand intermediation capacity, along with anonymity and lower counterparty risk. But these benefits require contributions to a default fund and higher margining. By June 2027, an SEC rule will require central clearing of U.S. Treasury-collateralized repos where at least one counterparty is a direct member of a registered clearing agency.

The implications vary by segment. Government money market funds, which account for over 80 percent of total U.S. MMF assets as of March 2026, are major cash lenders and typically settle through third-party agents to manage collateral and custody. Bilateral trades, favored by levered investors such as hedge funds, have grown significantly: hedge funds' outstanding repo volume reached $3 trillion in 2025:Q4, and bilateral activity rose to almost 40 percent of MMF repo activity in 2026:Q1. Rates are typically lower in this segment, with 75 percent of all Treasury repo trades in the bilateral market clearing at lower rates. Meanwhile, centrally cleared repo has expanded from 8 percent of activity in early 2021 to 30 percent in late 2025.

The analysis is bounded by its source base: a single New York Fed post read in full. It does not provide granular data on haircuts by collateral type, the distribution of maturities, or the behavior of non-Treasury collateral segments. The $13 trillion estimate is described as recent but not precisely dated, and the $200 billion comparison covers only selected deposit markets, not all short-term funding. What to watch is whether the June 2027 central clearing mandate shifts more bilateral activity into cleared venues, how dealer intermediation capacity responds to higher margining costs, and whether the growth of bilateral hedge fund repo alters the risk profile of money market funds that increasingly lend into that segment.
