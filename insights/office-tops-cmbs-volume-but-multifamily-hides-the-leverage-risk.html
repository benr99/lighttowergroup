Office Tops CMBS Volume, But Multifamily Hides the Leverage Risk

SASB deals drove office’s lead; conduit multifamily loans carry the highest leverage and thinnest debt yield.

Ben Rohr August 12, 2026 2 min read 1 source
Share LinkedIn X / Twitter

One chart, one argument

22.7% ($17.3 billion)Office share of issuance
8.20%Multifamily loan-level debt yield

Through July, domestic private-label CMBS hit $76.2 billion in issuance, and office was the surprise leader at 22.7% — $17.3 billion of loan balance. In a year still full of office bankruptcy headlines, that number looks like a contradiction. The explanation is not a sudden return to office love; it’s the concentrated power of the single-asset, single-borrower market.

SASB deals contributed $58.0 billion of the total, conduit just $16.1 billion. Office, industrial, and lodging together made up 62.1% of SASB. Data centers — an entirely SASB-only category — reached 9.8% of that channel. Conduit, by contrast, leaned on multifamily and retail. That channel split is the entire story.

The underwriting metrics confirm that lenders are not betting broadly. Office and retail loans carried LTVs near 59%, the lowest among major types, reflecting a cautious approach to value. The volume came from big-ticket trophies where sponsors are willing to pledge high-quality assets at conservative leverage. Meanwhile, multifamily — the darling of conduit — arrived with an 8.20% debt yield, the thinnest of any major type, and an LTV of 68.4%, the highest. Its debt-service coverage ratio was just 1.33x. Lodging, on the opposite end, required a 12.69% debt yield to compensate for operational volatility. In short, the most aggressively underwritten loans in 2026 are apartment loans, not office.

Look at the conduit book’s anatomy: LTV reached a post-pandemic high of 58.5%, but that’s only half the concern. By balance, 93.4% of those loans are interest-only for their entire term. Little principal is repaid before the balloon comes due. Multifamily borrowers have maximized their cash flow now but must refinance the full balance at maturity, whatever rates and values look like then.

The broad 2026 vintage’s 63.0% overall LTV is the highest reading in Trepp’s table, up from a 54.0% trough in 2023. Debt yield, at 10.36%, sits above the 8.77% of 2021 but below 2023’s 11.86%. Income coverage is firmer than at the last cycle peak even as leverage has returned. But that average masks the skew: office and large SASB deals provided the income cushion, while conduit multifamily stretched thin.

Data centers deserve notice. Only five loans underpin that data, so it’s early, but they’re new to the CMBS table, all in SASB. That suggests the capital markets are treating them as a distinct, financeable asset class — but only when each deal stands on its own. The channel exclusivity tells you as much about underwriting confidence as it does about the sector’s maturity.

The takeaway for a deal professional: Don’t be fooled by the office headline. The money went to office, but the betting went on apartments. Those 8.20% debt yields and interest-only structures won’t offer much cover if rent growth slows or cap rates tick up.
