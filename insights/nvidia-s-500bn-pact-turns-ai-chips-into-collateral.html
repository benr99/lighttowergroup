Nvidia’s $500bn pact turns AI chips into collateral

Six Wall Street firms commit capital as GPU-backed debt revives 1990s-style vendor financing risks

Ben Rohr August 12, 2026 3 min read 1 source

The formal announcement came only hours after a leaked report briefly knocked Nvidia’s share price. By the close of trading, the company had confirmed memoranda of understanding with Apollo Global Management, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield Asset Management, Goldman Sachs and KKR to mobilise more than $500 billion in third-party capital for AI infrastructure. Nvidia may backstop up to $125 billion of the total, underwriting roughly a quarter of the credit risk itself. The pact is not simply a large financing. It recasts Nvidia not as a hardware vendor but as the fulcrum on which a new asset class turns. Nvidia is no longer just selling the shovels; it is now underwriting the mine.

Under the arrangement, hyperscalers, cloud providers and AI labs will be able to borrow against Nvidia graphics processing units (GPUs) rather than paying for them upfront. The partner firms supply “dedicated pools of capital at significant scale at attractive rates,” according to the announcement. Jensen Huang, Nvidia’s founder and chief executive, framed the move in deliberately categoric terms during a joint CNBC interview with the partner firms: “We began by building chips; today, we are helping create a new class of productive, investable infrastructure: AI factories.”

The structure deliberately positions GPUs and the data centres that house them as long-life, revenue-producing assets — akin to toll roads or commercial property — rather than as rapidly depreciating IT equipment. That logic mirrors the $35 billion special-purpose vehicle that Apollo and Blackstone structured for Anthropic, which Capacity reported in June proved that chip-backed private credit could be priced at investment-grade rates. The new platform extends that template at wholesale, letting customers draw repeatedly on standing infrastructure rather than negotiating bespoke deals one by one.

Yet the pact also revives a pattern of circularity that veteran infrastructure lenders will recognise. When a dominant supplier backstops its own customers’ debt, the capital flow echoes the vendor-financing schemes that telecoms equipment makers such as Lucent and Nortel ran in the late 1990s. Those structures unravelled when customers defaulted and the collateral — then, networking hardware — proved to have far less resale value than underwriters had assumed. Nvidia’s architecture makes a similar concentrated bet: the hardware that the company sells becomes the security for the loans that fund its purchase.

Two immediate risks flow from that concentration. The first is collateral quality. GPU-backed loans assume that Nvidia hardware retains meaningful resale value across customer bases and silicon generations. Rapid architectural cycles challenge that assumption. A lender writing a 10-year facility against chips that may be three years old from the outset is modelling obsolescence timelines that no participant in the market has yet tested through a full credit cycle. The second is counterparty concentration. Each of the six financial partners is US-based, and the announcement is silent on how the facility will interact with sovereign and regional financing vehicles already active in Europe, the Gulf and Asia, where the bulk of near-term capacity expansion is concentrated. It also leaves open the question of whether the platform will be accessible to smaller colocation operators and developers or whether it will remain the preserve of hyperscalers and frontier labs.

What the pact most clearly marks is an industrialisation of AI infrastructure credit. Morgan Stanley has estimated that tech companies may need to raise up to $800 billion in private credit through asset-specific structures by 2028. JLL’s Global Data Centre Outlook puts total data centre investment at closer to $3 trillion over five years. Nvidia’s $500 billion platform, arriving through a single coordinated channel rather than the deal-by-deal patchwork that has characterised the market, lowers the barrier for AI companies without investment-grade balance sheets to secure debt on terms comparable to the hyperscalers. It does not, however, resolve what happens to that debt if AI demand growth slows before the silicon depreciates. For an industry already balancing capital costs against power availability, that unresolved question is the one that will define the next wave of deals.
