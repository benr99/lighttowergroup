RCS Founder Puts $50M of His Own Cash Behind $350M Office Distress Bet

The 42-year-old firm is using deep discounts and personal capital to call a bottom in office—but leverage cuts both ways.

Ben Rohr August 11, 2026 2 min read 1 source

When RCS founder Marcel Arsenault committed $50 million to the firm’s new distressed office fund, he wasn’t just seeding a $350 million vehicle. Arsenault didn’t just write a check for the fund—he wrote the first check, and it was his own $50 million. He was making a personal bet that the office market’s worst days are behind it.

The RCS Contrarian Office Fund, launched this week, aims to raise $350 million by the first quarter of 2027 and then use leverage to acquire up to $850 million in Class A and B office properties across the U.S. A separate, unnamed entrepreneur has already committed $47.5 million alongside Arsenault’s anchor check.

This is not a blind wager. Since 2024, RCS has already invested $644 million in 14 office properties across 10 markets. Among them: the 35-story Equitable Building in Chicago, bought earlier this year for $132.5 million—a 77.8% discount to its replacement cost. Other acquisitions include Walnut Glen Tower in Dallas and 101 Marietta Street in Atlanta.

“The whole office market today has been painted with the same brush, and the discounts go across the board, regardless if it’s trophy office or Class A, B or C buildings,” chief investment officer Adam Abeln said. His team’s contrarian playbook: enter markets at different points in their cycles, buying when pricing disconnects from what they view as fundamentals. Arsenault added that “companies still need high-quality workplaces to attract tenants.”

But the fund’s structure reveals the crucial role of borrowed money. Turning $350 million of equity into $850 million of buying power implies a debt load that could amplify both gains and losses. If office incomes continue to slide, leverage will punish returns just as quickly. The firm sold 80% of its portfolio ahead of the GFC, and since 2008 has generated a 24% return across 177 investments—including well-timed exits before COVID—suggesting a keen sense of market cycles. Today’s office distress, however, is partly demand-driven, with remote work permanently shifting space needs. It is not just a pricing glitch.

Arsenault’s personal check is a powerful demonstration of conviction. But it doesn’t answer the question every potential LP will ask: Is this 1992, when RCS profited from the S&L; crisis, or a structural reset where trophy offices trade at deep discounts because their income streams are permanently impaired? The fund has identified targets in at least 15 markets. The real test will be the cap rate they pay, the tenants they can retain, and whether the debt terms leave enough room for error in a recovery that remains stubbornly slow.
