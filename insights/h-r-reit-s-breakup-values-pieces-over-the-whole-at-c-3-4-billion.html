H&R REIT’s Breakup Values Pieces Over the Whole at C$3.4 Billion

A divided buyer pool pays a 14.5% premium to separate U.S. sunbelt apartments from Canadian industrial, revealing what the market won't price together.

Ben Rohr August 12, 2026 2 min read 1 source

A decade of underperformance ended with a decision to split the pieces. H&R; Real Estate Investment Trust, which spent years shedding office and retail holdings to refocus on North American apartments and industrial, agreed to be acquired in a breakup that values its equity at C$3.4 billion. The buyers didn't want the whole. They wanted specific slices.

GO Residential REIT will take the 23 U.S. sunbelt apartment properties, plus H&R;'s 50% stake in New York's Jackson Park luxury high-rise and other residential assets. A consortium of Blackstone, Crestpoint Real Estate Investments, and Canada's Public Sector Pension Investment Board gets the Canadian industrial portfolio. The Hofstedter family, which controls H&R;'s CEO, takes the remaining non-core assets. The transaction, including assumed debt, values the enterprise at roughly C$6.7 billion.

The price per unit—C$12.01, paid as C$4.28 in cash and 0.5688 GO REIT units—represents a 14.5% premium to H&R;'s closing price on June 10, before the deal was announced. The real message is in the structure: unitholders will end up owning 67% of the newly listed GO Residential, a pure-play sunbelt apartment vehicle they can hold or sell. The cash-and-stock split gives them the same choice the buyers made: retain exposure to the higher-growth residential segment or exit.

H&R;'s independent lead trustee, Stephen Gross, called the transaction the product of an exhaustive review conducted last year. That review was forced by a painful reality. The REIT had failed to close the gap between its portfolio's underlying value and what the public market was willing to pay. The restructuring into apartments and industrial was directionally right, but the market still penalized the overhang of legacy assets and the conglomerate structure. Breaking the company apart—and letting separate buyers with sector-specific capital set the price—unblocked that value.

The buyers are paying a premium, but not an unsupported one. The deal reflects continued investor demand for industrial properties and sunbelt multifamily housing, with institutional capital willing to pay premiums for stabilized assets. Neither transaction appears priced at a temporary discount; these are full bids for assets that each buyer intends to hold and operate, not flip. The 14.5% premium suggests not a bargain, but the removal of a conglomerate discount that had weighed on H&R; for years.

The risk edges lie on the other side. Unitholders will own two-thirds of GO Residential, which listed on the Toronto Stock Exchange last year and has a short public track record. A sudden repricing in sunbelt multifamily values would flow directly into the unit price. For now, the market's initial reaction appears to accept that trade: the deal price reflects the value of the parts, not the whole.

The breakup also reveals a quiet ceiling in Canadian public real estate. H&R; couldn't convince the market to pay the sum of its pieces while it remained a single, diversified entity. Other REITs with similar conglomerate profiles will watch this closely. The point isn't that sunbelt multifamily is suddenly worth more; it's that separating it from everything else allowed a buyer to act on a conviction the public market couldn't articulate. The test now is whether GO Residential's units can sustain a premium once the deal closes, or whether the breakup premium was the peak.
