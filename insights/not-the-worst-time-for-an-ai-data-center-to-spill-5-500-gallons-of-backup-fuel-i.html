Not the worst time for an AI data center to spill 5,500 gallons of backup fuel into a tributary

An Equinix data center in New Jersey dumped 5,500 gallons of diesel into a Hackensack River tributary after a computer glitch, while the EPA moved to repeal pow

Ben Rohr September 21, 2026 1 min read 1 source

Earlier this month, a "computer glitch" at an Equinix data center in New Jersey prompted 5,500 gallons of diesel to be dumped into a tributary of the Hackensack River. The fuel came from a storage tank that powers backup generators at the site, which has been active since 2001. The Department of Environmental Protection said no harm to wildlife has been reported, but local officials are skeptical the spill won't have an environmental impact. The incident is currently under investigation.

The spill arrived as the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency followed through on its promise to repeal rules limiting emissions from coal- and gas-fired power plants. The EPA is further proposing to repeal all remaining greenhouse gas emissions standards for fossil-fired facilities under Section 111 of the Clean Air Act, claiming it has no authority to regulate power plant emissions based on their contribution to climate change. The EPA estimates the final repeal will save the industry up to $310 billion. However, an Associated Press examination found that maintaining the rules targeted by Trump's EPA prevents an estimated 30,000 deaths per year and saves $275 billion annually.

The timing matters because climate concerns have taken a backseat to speed-to-power discussions in the United States. More power plants will be allowed to stay open longer without procuring expensive upgrades at a time when every electron is critical. The spill at the Equinix facility illustrates the physical risks of this acceleration: backup fuel systems supporting AI infrastructure can fail in ways that directly threaten local ecosystems, even when no wildlife harm is immediately reported.

What remains unknown is the full environmental impact of the New Jersey spill and whether local officials' skepticism will be validated. The investigation is ongoing, and the source does not report any penalties, remediation costs, or long-term monitoring plans. It is also unclear how the EPA's proposed repeal of Section 111 standards will interact with state-level environmental enforcement or future litigation over the agency's authority to regulate power plant emissions.
