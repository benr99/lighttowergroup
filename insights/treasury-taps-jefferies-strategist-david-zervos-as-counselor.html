Treasury taps Jefferies strategist David Zervos as counselor

The Treasury Department has hired longtime Wall Street strategist David Zervos as a counselor to Secretary Scott Bessent, adding a vocal advocate for lower rate

Ben Rohr September 28, 2026 3 min read 1 source
Share LinkedIn X / Twitter

The Treasury Department has hired longtime Wall Street strategist David Zervos as a counselor in Secretary Scott Bessent's office, a move that places a prominent advocate for lower interest rates and expanded long-term debt buybacks inside the department at a moment when long-dated Treasury yields remain elevated and questions persist over the boundary between monetary policy and debt management. Zervos, most recently chief market strategist at Jefferies LLC, brings 35 years of experience across global financial markets, central banking, and macroeconomic analysis, according to Treasury's announcement. The hiring matters because it signals the department is adding internal capacity to shape and defend its debt-management strategy as it fights against elevated long-term yields and broader concerns about government debt.

The material facts are narrow but concrete. Treasury said Zervos previously advised leading money managers, banks, hedge funds, pension funds, and sovereign wealth funds, and he also spent time as an advisor in the Federal Reserve Board's Division of Monetary Affairs. Treasury plans to buy up to $6 billion of older 10- and 20-year Treasury securities, a buyback effort that Bessent has framed as a market-making function for less liquid off-the-run bonds. Zervos has publicly supported that approach, describing the buybacks in August as a tactical move similar to past operation twists that have been successful when the Fed initiated them, while noting it is more unusual for the Treasury to do so.

The evidence comes from a single American Banker report read in full, which draws on Treasury's announcement and Zervos's prior public comments. Zervos has argued that Federal Reserve monetary policy has become overly restrictive, saying that keeping interest rates high and the Fed balance sheet small has restrained the economy, and he has called for rates to move toward a lower, more neutral level while warning about unnecessarily weakening demand and the labor market. He has also pushed back on skepticism of the department's actions to ease long-term bond yields, saying he does not see how one could fight the buybacks when the firepower and the cards are all sitting in the Treasury Department. On the $40 trillion deficit figures, Zervos said people are getting a little hot and bothered and suggested the buyback was an opportune moment to put a kibosh on the potential for a runaway through the top end of the range.

The sector and market implications center on the Treasury yield curve and the interaction between debt management and monetary policy. By adding a counselor who has publicly called for lower rates and endorsed buybacks of longer-term debt, Treasury is reinforcing its focus on the long end of the curve at a time when long-dated yields are a source of pressure. The buyback program, while modest at up to $6 billion, is positioned as a liquidity and spread-management tool for off-the-run securities rather than a large-scale intervention. Still, the hiring may be read by market participants as a signal that Treasury intends to continue using debt-management tools to influence long-term borrowing conditions, even as the Federal Reserve retains control over the policy rate and balance sheet.

The main limitation is that the dossier reflects a single source read in full, and several material details remain unknown. The report does not specify the exact start date, the scope of Zervos's advisory responsibilities, or whether his views will translate into concrete policy changes beyond the existing buyback plan. It also does not provide independent corroboration of the $6 billion buyback figure or the $40 trillion deficit reference beyond the source text. What to watch is whether Zervos's public stance on restrictive Fed policy influences Treasury's messaging or coordination with the central bank, and whether the buyback program expands or shifts in response to long-term yield movements.
