Nvidia’s $500bn pact turns GPUs into infrastructure collateral

The chipmaker and six financiers are creating a credit platform that could reshape how AI capacity is funded—and who bears the risk when hardware ages.

Nvidia’s chips are no longer just the engines of AI—they are becoming the collateral for the data centres that house them. In mid-August, the company signed memoranda of understanding with Apollo Global Management, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield Asset Management, Goldman Sachs and KKR to mobilise more than $500 billion in third-party capital for AI infrastructure. Nvidia may backstop up to $125 billion of that total, roughly a quarter, absorbing a slice of the credit risk directly.

Rather than customers paying upfront for racks of GPUs and the power to run them, the new platform will let hyperscalers, cloud providers and AI labs borrow against the hardware itself. The six financial firms have committed to providing “dedicated pools of capital at significant scale at attractive rates,” according to the announcement. The shift reframes GPUs less like rapidly depreciating IT equipment and more like toll roads or commercial property—assets with a long, financeable revenue tail. “We began by building chips; today, we are helping create a new class of productive, investable infrastructure: AI factories,” Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang said in a joint CNBC interview with the partner firms’ executives.

The scarce resource being addressed here is capital. Morgan Stanley has estimated that tech firms and their ecosystem could need to raise up to $800 billion in private credit through asset-specific structures by 2028. JLL’s Global Data Centre Outlook puts total data centre investment at closer to $3 trillion over five years. Nvidia’s $500 billion facility, arriving through a single coordinated channel rather than deal-by-deal negotiation, represents about a sixth of the likely five-year requirement. That scale, and the fact that Nvidia—not a borrower—is the counterparty setting terms, turns a patchwork of bespoke financing into an industrialised credit engine.

The structure, however, embeds assumptions about collateral quality that no one has fully tested. GPU-backed lending depends on the belief that Nvidia hardware will retain resale value across multiple customer generations. Rapid architecture cycles complicate that bet; a lender underwriting a 10-year facility against silicon that may be three generations old in five years is wagering on obsolescence timelines the industry cannot yet model. There is also concentration risk. As Axios noted, a financing system in which the dominant chip supplier also backstops its customers’ borrowing revives uncomfortable memories of vendor-financing schemes from the late-1990s telecoms boom, when Lucent and Nortel extended credit that soured when their customers collapsed. For now, Nvidia’s pact leaves interest rates, individual firm commitments and a deployment schedule undisclosed. It also says nothing about how smaller colocation operators or developers outside the US might access the pools.

The industrialisation of GPU-backed debt does not resolve the questions about counterparty concentration, maturity mismatches or the nerve-wracking gap between debt tenors and silicon shelf lives. It does, however, make Nvidia the fulcrum on which those questions now turn. The next signal to watch is whether lenders begin to price risk by chip generation, and whether the secondary market for used GPUs can sustain the values that loan-to-value ratios assume when the first wave of this debt comes up for renewal.
