NVIDIA and Wall Street’s Biggest Landlords Try to Turn GPUs Into a $500B Asset Class

Six MOUs aim to finance AI factories the way airports and cell towers are funded—but power, not capital, may remain the real constraint.

Ben Rohr August 12, 2026 3 min read 1 source

For the past three years, the generative-AI buildout has been financed the way a homeowner pays for a renovation—cash on the barrelhead from hyperscaler balance sheets, supplemented by venture-backed neoclouds and one-off project finance deals. That era is now being deliberately brought to an end. NVIDIA has signed memorandums of understanding with six of the world’s largest alternative-asset managers—Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs, and KKR—to create dedicated financing platforms that aim to mobilize more than $500 billion of third-party capital for AI infrastructure.

The ambition is not simply to write a bigger check. It is to create a repeatable, standardized financial structure around AI compute itself, treating GPU clusters, networking, storage, power delivery, and cooling as a single productive infrastructure asset class, analogous to toll roads, cell towers, or power plants. The reason is straightforward: the physical scale of what NVIDIA calls “AI factories” is moving from tens to hundreds of megawatts and, in some planning corridors, toward the gigawatt level. The capital required no longer fits comfortably on any single tenant’s corporate balance sheet, even a hyperscaler’s.

The new platforms would allow institutional investors—pension funds, infrastructure funds, private-credit arms—to underwrite compute infrastructure based on the contracted or predictable usage that generates revenue from training and inference. “In AI, compute is revenue,” NVIDIA CEO Jensen Huang said. “NVIDIA compute is uniquely suited for this role.” Goldman Sachs went a step further, explicitly referencing the creation of a market for credit backed by NVIDIA compute, the way mortgages are backed by homes or power-purchase agreements by wind farms.

Yet the financial engineering bumps into a hard physical fact: every AI factory is, at bottom, a power infrastructure project with a roof. The unit of economic productivity—tokens per watt—is constrained by utility interconnection queues, transmission upgrade timelines, and the availability of behind-the-meter generation. Brookfield, which this year committed up to $9 billion toward expanding NAVER’s NVIDIA-based AI factory in Korea, already works at the intersection of power and digital infrastructure. BlackRock’s AI Infrastructure Partnership similarly targets data centers and their supporting power assets. The new NVIDIA MOUs take this one layer deeper, focusing on the compute layer, but they do not solve for the fundamental physical constraint of power delivery, which remains a separate, multi-year challenge.

What the initiative does change is the cost and availability of capital for AI infrastructure operators who can secure power. Lambda, which provides GPU cloud services, secured a $1 billion syndicated credit facility in May based on its NVIDIA gear. IREN has been pursuing GPU financing alongside major equipment purchases. These early deals hint at the possibility that a GPU cluster with high utilization and a software stack that locks in customers can be underwritten much like a contracted renewable-energy asset. The risk, of course, is that the assets depreciate on a curve far steeper than a cell tower or a transmission line. The residual value of an H100 cluster in 2029 is an open question that no MOU can answer.

The $500 billion figure should be read as an aspirational mobilization target over time, not committed capital. The MOUs remain subject to final agreements. The strategic direction, however, is unmistakable: NVIDIA wants pension and insurance capital to become a permanent scaling mechanism for AI infrastructure, just as it did for real estate and energy decades ago. Whether the capital arrives before the power does will determine which AI factories actually get built.
