Fed policymakers saw multiple 2026 rate hikes as likely, September FOMC minutes show

September FOMC minutes show most participants expected another rate increase by year-end, but softer inflation and weak jobs data have pushed market odds toward

Ben Rohr October 08, 2026 2 min read 1 source
Share LinkedIn X / Twitter

The September Federal Open Market Committee minutes, released Wednesday, show that when Fed officials raised interest rates by a quarter of a percentage point, most also expected another hike before the end of 2026. The minutes state that "most participants assessed that another increase in the target range for the federal funds rate would likely be appropriate by year end." That forward-looking signal matters because it frames the central bank's tightening bias even as incoming data have begun to soften, leaving markets to reconcile the committee's stated inclination with a rapidly shifting economic picture.

The minutes reveal a committee united on the need to address inflation but divided on the rationale. Some participants viewed the September move as a "risk management" action, providing "insurance against inflation remaining persistently above target due to stronger-than-expected demand or further adverse supply shocks." Others argued a higher policy rate would help prevent sector-specific price increases from energy market disruptions and AI-related demand from broadening into more persistent inflation dynamics. Several participants also said the current policy rate was "not restrictive or only mildly restrictive," suggesting the committee saw room to tighten further without overcorrecting.

The evidence base for the Fed's stance has weakened since the September meeting. Fed staffers had projected headline personal consumption expenditures inflation of 3.6% on a 12-month basis and core inflation of 3.2%, but government data released two weeks after the rate decision showed headline PCE at 3.4% and core at 3%. Both figures came in below consensus estimates, as economists polled by Reuters and Dow Jones had forecast 3.7% headline and 3.3% core. The labor market also deteriorated: U.S. employers added just 29,000 nonfarm jobs in September, well below consensus forecasts above 80,000, and the Bureau of Labor Statistics downwardly revised a combined 60,000 jobs from the prior two months.

These data points have shifted market pricing. According to CME FedWatch, the probability of an October rate hike fell to 17% as of Wednesday, while the odds of a December hike rose to 84%. The October FOMC meeting concludes Oct. 28, and the central bank's dual mandate to support stable prices and full employment gives it reason to pause. The minutes and subsequent data suggest the committee's year-end tightening bias remains intact, but the path to another hike now depends on whether inflation and employment readings stabilize or weaken further.

One additional factor may influence timing: midterm elections in November. While the Federal Reserve operates outside the political sphere, it has historically been careful to avoid the appearance of influencing the political narrative. Rate changes just before a national election have tended to be controversial, which could reinforce the market's expectation that any further adjustment is more likely in December than in October. The minutes themselves do not address election timing, but the calendar context is part of the broader policy environment.

The main limitation of this analysis is that it rests on a single secondary source summarizing the minutes, without the full FOMC transcript or dissenting views. The minutes capture the committee's internal debate but not the intensity of disagreement or how individual policymakers weighted the competing risks. What to watch next is whether upcoming inflation and employment data confirm the softer trend, and whether the committee's year-end guidance holds or shifts in the October statement.
