Citi Buys a Way to Shift Reward Costs to Merchants

The Kard deal moves card rewards from issuer-funded expense to merchant-funded marketing.

Ben Rohr August 15, 2026 2 min read 1 source

The pending Citi–Kard transaction is less about buying rewards software than about changing who funds the reward. Citi, a $2.9 trillion-asset bank, has agreed to acquire New York-based commerce media and rewards fintech Kard Financial for an undisclosed amount. The purchase would connect Citi’s 70 million cardmembers to merchants that pay for targeted offers instead of relying solely on Citi’s own reward budget.

Abhinav Anand, Citi’s head of value cards, lending and commerce, said Kard adds personalization that identifies spending patterns and matches customers with relevant offers in real time. In practice, Citi can present rewards based on actual purchase behavior and give brands a direct channel to consumers whose spending patterns match the offer. That changes the economics. Under the legacy model, the issuer funds points to motivate spending. Under the Kard model, a merchant’s marketing budget funds the incentive, and Citi earns from the resulting payment volume and receivables.

Payments consultant Phil Philliou described the shift in P&L; terms: “Rewards points programs are a direct cost center that Citi funds as opposed to merchant-funded, offer-based rewards that shift that cost to brands who want targeted access to Citi’s 70 million cardmembers.” That is the central transfer: reward expense becomes merchant marketing expense. For Citi, the acquisition is a mechanism to buy merchant relationships and a matching engine rather than build them internally.

The risk does not disappear; it moves. Eric Grover, principal at Intrepid Ventures, said successful execution would generate incremental sales and customers for merchants. For Citi, it would generate incremental customers, payment volume and receivables. Those gains would boost interchange and finance revenue. That is Citi’s compensation for bearing integration and execution risk. But the merchant bears the initial cost of funding the incentive, and its payoff depends on proving that the offer produced a sale that would not otherwise have occurred. Aaron McPherson, principal at AFM Consulting, said the main problem with merchant-funded rewards has always been distinguishing transactions that would have happened anyway from net new transactions.

The undisclosed deal value leaves an important question unanswered: how much Citi is paying to acquire Kard’s predictive AI, first-party transaction data and merchant network. If Kard’s machine learning cannot solve the attribution problem, merchant-funded offers may simply subsidize existing cardholder spend, compressing merchant margins without reducing Citi’s underlying reward cost. The acquisition would still give Citi richer purchase data and a direct merchant channel, but not necessarily a lower structural cost.

The next observable test is whether Citi reports merchant-funded rewards as a measurable offset to reward expense or as a separate marketing capability inside the cards P&L.; Under continued interchange pressure, the bank has reason to move reward economics away from issuer-funded expense. But until Citi demonstrates incremental volume and receivables from the Kard network, the transaction has shifted the unresolved question—who pays for the reward and who can prove it worked—toward merchants.
