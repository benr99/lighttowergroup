Denver Offers a Tale of Two Downtowns

Union Station's $55 million revitalization turned LoDo into a thriving hub, but Upper Downtown's 40% office vacancy shows the recovery is uneven. Public acquisi

Ben Rohr October 06, 2026 3 min read 1 source
Share LinkedIn X / Twitter

Denver's downtown is splitting into two distinct economic stories. The 2014 reopening of Union Station after a $55 million public-private revitalization is widely credited with transforming Lower Downtown, or LoDo, into a dining, retail, and residential success. Yet just north, Upper Downtown is struggling, with office vacancy around 40%, according to Andrew Iltis, senior vice president of planning and community impact at Downtown Denver Partnership. The contrast matters because it shows how a single catalytic investment can reshape one district while leaving an adjacent core in distress, and it frames the city's current strategy of using targeted public acquisitions and small activations to replicate LoDo's momentum.

The mechanics of the divide are visible in the assets themselves. Union Station, owned by the city of Denver and RTD, was awarded in 2011 to the Union Station Alliance, which includes Sage, Urban Neighborhoods, McWhinney, REGen LLC, and Larimer Associates. A decade after the initial revitalization, another $12 million was invested in renovating the station's Great Hall and the Crawford Hotel guest rooms. More than 10,000 people now pass through the station daily, and it is sometimes called "Denver's living room." Meanwhile, Upper Downtown's recovery is being pursued through a $175 million multiyear revitalization of 16th Street, a 16-block pedestrian corridor that fully reopened last October with improvements to safety, mobility, infrastructure, and transit configuration.

The evidence for the two-downtown narrative comes from a single full-text source: a NAIOP-published account of a CREDA Conference walking tour, dated October 6, 2026. The source quotes Ed Blair, area general manager for Sage Hospitality, describing the Union Station transformation as "pretty remarkable," and Iltis stating, "It's the story of two downtowns. Our vision is to mimic and bring the story of LoDo to Upper Downtown." The report also cites Kim Sperry of Sperry Advisory, a consultant for the Denver Downtown Development Authority, who said Denver Pavilions is about 65% occupied after the pandemic and long-term 16th Street construction delivered a "one-two punch." The development authority bought Denver Pavilions at the end of 2025 for $45 million and acquired two on-site parking lots from Brookfield Properties for $23 million, creating potential for future large-scale mixed-use development.

For commercial real estate, the implications are twofold. First, the public sector is stepping in as an owner and assembler of distressed or underperforming assets, as shown by the Denver Downtown Development Authority's $68 million in combined acquisitions. Sperry noted that "the true redevelopment potential is on those parking lots," with a current vision involving vertical development, whether multifamily or a hotel, that reconnects to retail at the Pavilions. Second, the strategy is deliberately incremental. Iltis said Downtown Denver Partnership realized there was not enough market energy to change all of Upper Downtown at once, so the organization identified "catalytic nodes" such as Glenarm Plaza, a food market and public plaza where activation efforts led people to gather and later host the "Skynets Over Downtown" art installation. Skyline Park is also being reimagined, with Block 2 due next year to include splash pads, a water feature, and beer garden-style kiosks.

The main limitation is that the dossier reflects one secondary source read in full, with no independent corroboration of the cited figures or occupancy rates. The source does not provide historical vacancy data for Upper Downtown, comparable LoDo office metrics, or details on financing terms for the acquisitions. It also does not specify the timeline for vertical development on the parking lots or the expected cost of the Skyline Park and Glenarm Plaza activations. What to watch is whether the catalytic-node approach produces measurable new investment in Upper Downtown, whether the Pavilions occupancy improves before any redevelopment begins, and whether the 16th Street corridor sustains foot traffic now that construction has ended. The success of Union Station suggests the model can work, but the 40% vacancy figure is a reminder that replication is not guaranteed.
