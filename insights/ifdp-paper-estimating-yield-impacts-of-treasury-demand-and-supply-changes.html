IFDP Paper: Estimating Yield Impacts of Treasury Demand and Supply Changes

A new Federal Reserve working paper quantifies how shifts in Treasury supply and demand affect yields, finding the market has become more price-sensitive as for

Ben Rohr September 19, 2026 2 min read 1 source
Share LinkedIn X / Twitter

A new International Finance Discussion Paper from Federal Reserve staff economists Daniel Beltran and Canlin Li develops a demand system framework to quantify how shifts in U.S. Treasury supply and demand move yields. The finding that a $100 billion increase in Treasury supply currently raises five-year yields by approximately 3 basis points matters because it gives policymakers and market participants a concrete, current estimate of the yield impact of supply changes at a time when Treasury issuance and investor composition are both shifting. The paper also argues that the Treasury market has become increasingly price-sensitive over time, a structural change with implications for how smoothly new issuance is absorbed and how sensitive yields are to demand shocks.

The model captures time-varying holdings shares across investor sectors and estimates sectoral demand elasticities using instrumental variables. The key mechanical driver identified in the paper is the changing composition of the investor base: less price-sensitive foreign official investors have declined in participation, while more price-sensitive hedge funds and other private investors have risen in importance. This shift in who holds Treasuries is what makes the market more responsive to supply changes. The authors validate the model by showing that shifts in the investor base explain a significant portion of historical yield changes, which supports the framework's usefulness for counterfactual analysis.

The evidence comes from a single primary source: the Federal Reserve Working Papers page for IFDP 2026.1447, published in September 2026. The abstract is the full evidentiary boundary for this analysis, and it explicitly frames the framework as a flexible tool for policy analysis and counterfactual scenarios, including the yield effects of foreign official investor sales and Federal Reserve balance sheet policies. The paper's keywords include balance sheet policies, Treasury markets, asset pricing, demand elasticity, foreign official sales, and term premium. The authors note that the research represents their own views and does not indicate concurrence by other Board staff or the Board of Governors, and that conclusions are often preliminary and circulated to stimulate discussion.

For market participants, the 3-basis-point estimate for a $100 billion supply increase provides a benchmark for evaluating issuance announcements, auction sizes, and quantitative tightening or easing scenarios. The finding that hedge funds and other private investors are now a larger, more price-sensitive share of demand suggests that yield reactions to supply news may be larger or faster than in earlier periods dominated by foreign official buyers. The framework's ability to model foreign official sales is also relevant if reserve managers reduce Treasury holdings, since the paper implies such sales would have a measurable yield impact through the same demand elasticity channels.

The main limitation is that the dossier contains only the abstract, not the full paper, so the exact identification strategy, sample period, and sectoral elasticity estimates are not available. The 3-basis-point figure is specific to five-year yields and current market conditions, and the paper does not provide a range or confidence interval in the abstract. What to watch is whether the framework's counterfactual scenarios are adopted in policy discussions around Federal Reserve balance sheet normalization or foreign official reserve reallocation, and whether subsequent research corroborates the claim that investor base shifts explain a significant portion of historical yield changes.
