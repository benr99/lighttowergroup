ARENA funds UNSW grid-forming battery fault-current test

AU$6.52m lab program targets the protection-grade fault-current gap AEMO says still blocks BESS from system strength.

A 38% cost blowout in synchronous condenser projects has turned an inverter fault-current question into a commercial decision for Australian transmission owners. The Australian Renewable Energy Agency has awarded UNSW AU$6.52 million (US$4.24 million) to test whether grid-forming battery storage can deliver protection-quality fault current, not just voltage waveform stability.

The project, named Protection & Relay Operation for Inverter-based Low-inertia Electricity Systems (PROFILES), will run until November 2030 and is led by Associate Professor Georgios Konstantinou from UNSW’s School of Electrical Engineering and Telecommunications. Hardware-in-the-loop testing at the university’s Real Time Simulations Laboratory will examine how solar, wind and battery storage interact with existing protection systems in the National Electricity Market. Industry partners include Transgrid, AEMO, ElectraNet, Power Electronics, SMA, Tesla and Siemens.

AEMO’s 2026 General Power System Risk Review has already defined what is missing: grid-forming battery energy storage has not yet been shown to meet the fault-current standard that the National Electricity Rules set for minimum system strength, even though it has demonstrated voltage waveform support. The work responds to that gap in AEMO’s Transition Plan for System Security.

That finding has commercial weight. Grid-forming inverters appear in 74% of Australia’s 33.2GW NEM battery storage pipeline. Transmission businesses have considered substituting battery storage for planned synchronous condenser installations after the 38% condenser cost blowout, and AEMO has proposed a Type 2 Transitional Services procurement to trial the technology under real-world grid conditions. A positive technical result would give grid-forming BESS developers a route into contracted system strength revenue; an unfavourable result would preserve demand for synchronous condensers.

The unresolved question is whether protection engineers will accept inverter fault current as equivalent to rotating machine fault current. If they do, battery storage could compete for the system strength role now filled by synchronous condensers; if they do not, transmission owners will keep paying for rotating machines.
