NYSERDA approves 1.7 GW of new state renewable energy projects

New York's energy authority is initiating eight bulk storage projects and 13 large-scale renewables, expected to deliver 1.7 GW of capacity, more than 1,000 job

Ben Rohr September 24, 2026 3 min read 1 source
Share LinkedIn X / Twitter

The New York State Energy Research and Development Authority (NYSERDA) is initiating eight new bulk energy storage projects and 13 large-scale renewable energy projects across the state. Combined, the projects are expected to provide 1.7 GW of new large-scale energy resources, create more than 1,000 family-supporting jobs, and generate over $3.7 billion in private investment. The announcement matters because it signals a material expansion of New York's contracted clean energy pipeline at a time when grid reliability and ratepayer costs remain central policy concerns. NYSERDA President and CEO Doreen M. Harris framed the approvals as part of a broader strategy, saying the projects "not only further the growth of clean energy and more than double the utility-scale energy storage that exists currently statewide, but they contribute to New York's comprehensive, all of the above energy strategy."

The approvals stem from two separate solicitations: NYSERDA's Bulk Energy Storage solicitation and its 2025 Tier 1 Renewable Energy Standard solicitation. The eight contracted bulk energy storage projects will add 950 MW of new storage capacity, with durations ranging from four to eight hours. The 13 contracted large-scale renewable projects will total more than 719 MW of capacity, enough to power over 230,000 New York homes annually once completed. The storage portfolio is geographically diverse, spanning New York City, Long Island, the Hudson Valley, the Finger Lakes, and western New York. Named projects include Lighthouse Energy Storage in Chautauqua County, Zenobe Burns in Allegany County, Eastwater Energy Storage in the Finger Lakes region, KCE NY 5 in the Hudson Valley, Blue Spruce Storage and Palladium Storage in Bronx County, and Holtsville and KCE NY 37 on Long Island.

The source material provides several concrete financial and procedural details. The projects are expected to provide over $127 million in economic activity in disadvantaged communities, as defined by the Climate Justice Working Group. Payments under the storage contracts will not commence until projects have begun commercial operation, after obtaining all permits and local approvals. The storage projects were selected from a competitive process that included bid proposals from 46 projects totaling approximately 6 GW of capacity, indicating a substantial pipeline relative to the 950 MW ultimately contracted. The contracted storage projects will use lithium-ion technology and are contractually required to meet new storage safety codes that became effective January 1, 2026. NYSERDA also said it will issue a second annual Bulk Energy Storage Request for Proposals by year-end, with plans to procure 3 GW of energy storage projects over three solicitations.

The sector implications are significant for New York's energy storage and renewable development markets. The 950 MW of new storage capacity would more than double the utility-scale energy storage currently operating statewide, according to Harris. The named solar projects include Flint Mine Solar, Hecate Greene 1 and 2, Greens Corners Solar Park, and Rich Road Solar Energy Center. All projects are expected to be placed in-service by the end of 2030, as required by the 2024 New York Public Service Commission's Energy Storage Order. For developers, the structure of payments beginning only at commercial operation shifts permitting and construction risk onto project sponsors, while the multi-solicitation procurement plan suggests continued demand for storage capacity beyond this initial tranche.

The analysis is limited by the single-source nature of the evidence. The dossier relies on one secondary source, Solar Power World, which republished a news item from NYSERDA. The source does not include project-level contract prices, individual capacity figures for most renewable projects, developer identities beyond project names, or the specific timeline for each project's commercial operation date. It also does not provide detail on how the $127 million in disadvantaged community economic activity was calculated or over what period. What to watch includes the second annual Bulk Energy Storage RFP expected by year-end, progress on permitting and local approvals for the named projects, and whether the contracted projects meet their in-service deadlines by the end of 2030.
