Valley National Bank to acquire fintech Bluevine for $340M

The cash-and-stock deal is set to bring Valley $2.1 billion in deposits and a 20-fold expansion of its small-business customer base, executives said Monday.

Ben Rohr September 28, 2026 3 min read 1 source
Share LinkedIn X / Twitter

Valley National Bancorp has agreed to acquire Bluevine, a Jersey City-based small-business banking fintech, in a cash-and-stock deal valued at $340 million, the bank said Monday. The transaction matters because it represents a deliberate attempt by a $66 billion-asset regional bank to reorient its funding profile and accelerate its relevance in small-business banking. Valley CEO Ira Robbins framed the deal as a way to make the bank “a much stronger and more relevant small business competitor,” while also addressing what he called “the core funding challenge that we see today at Valley.”

The mechanics of the deal are specific and consequential. Bluevine, founded in 2013, has about 175,000 active small-business customers and is set to bring Valley approximately $2.1 billion in deposits. Those deposits are currently held at partner bank Coastal Community Bank but will transfer to Valley in the first half of 2027, according to Robbins. The consideration is 75% cash and 25% Valley stock. Valley expects about 8% earnings per share accretion and about 5% tangible book value dilution at closing, with a three-year earnback period. The deal is projected to close early in 2027.

The evidence comes from a single full-text report by Banking Dive, published September 28, 2026, which draws on Valley's announcement and a Monday conference call with executives. The report cites specific figures from an investor presentation, including Bluevine's 1.44% cost of deposits compared with Valley's 2.28%. Robbins said the incoming deposits will improve the bank's funding mix and reduce reliance on higher-cost wholesale funding. Bluevine co-founder and CEO Eyal Lifshitz, who will join Valley as head of small-business banking, said the deal “felt like, for us, the best option to accelerate our vision of building our small business franchise.” The report also notes that Bluevine has invested almost $200 million in its digital acquisition platform and user experience, and that account opening takes about five minutes.

The sector implications are twofold. First, the deal illustrates a convergence pattern in which fintechs seek the balance sheet access, stability, and credibility of a regulated banking environment, while lenders seek the “proven digital acquisition channels, proprietary technology and modern customer experiences” that fintechs have cultivated, as Robbins put it. Second, Valley is absorbing Bluevine's technology and AI strategy, including roughly 180 research-and-development employees and engineers. Robbins said the majority of the fintech's code is AI-generated and about 80% of inbound client inquiries are resolved by AI. The bank also sees cross-sell potential: about 40% of Bluevine's customers are within Valley's footprint, creating opportunities for treasury management, wealth, and other services.

The evidence base has clear limitations. The dossier relies on one secondary source, and no primary regulatory filing or independent financial analysis was available to corroborate the stated accretion, dilution, or deposit figures. The report does not include Bluevine's revenue, profitability, or loan book composition, nor does it detail regulatory approval requirements or integration risks. J.P. Morgan Securities analyst Anthony Elian wrote that neither this deal nor Valley's separate $247 million acquisition of Providence Financial Corp. is expected to disrupt Valley's organic growth profile “given the relatively small size of both deals,” but that assessment is a single analyst view. What to watch is whether the deposit transfer completes on schedule in the first half of 2027, whether the 1.44% deposit cost holds after migration, and whether Valley can convert Bluevine's national digital acquisition platform into sustained small-business relationship growth without additional acquisitions, which Robbins said the bank does not anticipate for the foreseeable future.
