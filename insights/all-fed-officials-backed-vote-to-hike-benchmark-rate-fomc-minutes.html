All Fed officials backed vote to hike benchmark rate: FOMC minutes

Minutes from the September FOMC meeting show unanimous support among all 19 Federal Reserve officials for the quarter-point rate increase, while rising consumer

Ben Rohr October 08, 2026 2 min read 1 source
Share LinkedIn X / Twitter

The Federal Reserve's September policy decision drew unanimous backing from all 19 officials, not just the 12 voting members of the Federal Open Market Committee, according to minutes released Wednesday. The quarter-point increase in the main interest rate reflected a shared assessment that the economy and labor market remained stable, but also an imperative to bring inflation back to the Fed's 2% goal. The breadth of support matters because it signals that the central bank's inflation-fighting resolve is not confined to a narrow majority, even as subsequent data and public comments have introduced uncertainty about the timing of any further hikes.

The mechanics of the decision are straightforward but consequential. The FOMC's 12 policymakers voted unanimously on Sept. 28 to raise the target range for the federal funds rate by a quarter point. The minutes also show that the seven regional bank presidents who rotate onto the committee but were not currently voting members supported the move. Looking ahead, most participants assessed that another increase would likely be appropriate by year end, though they emphasized that future decisions would depend on incoming information. This forward guidance is deliberately conditional, leaving the Fed room to pause if the data weaken.

The evidence base for the Fed's concern is visible in the New York Fed's survey data released the same day. Median inflation expectations among consumers rose 0.3 percentage point to 3.9% for the one-year horizon and 0.1 percentage point to 3.3% for the three-year horizon. Five-year expectations were unchanged at 3%. The minutes captured this anxiety directly: some participants expressed concerns that after more than five years of inflation above 2%, elevated inflation rates could begin to affect inflation expectations and wage- and price-setting decisions. Some participants also remarked that a higher policy rate would diminish the risk of persistently elevated inflation un-anchoring expectations and becoming further entrenched.

The market implications are already visible in rate futures. Traders reduced the odds of a hike this month to 17.2% from 37.6% a week earlier, according to CME Group's FedWatch tool, after Fed Vice Chair Philip Jefferson and New York Fed President John Williams signaled no urgency to raise borrowing costs and a weak jobs report showed only 29,000 jobs created in September. The unemployment rate edged up to 4.2%. Yet the minutes reveal a split in tone: Dallas Fed President Lori Logan argued that strong growth and resilient consumer spending are signs monetary policy is not restrictive, and she favored additional increases. This tension between dovish public signals and hawkish internal sentiment leaves the near-term policy path genuinely uncertain.

The main limitation of this evidence is that it captures a single meeting's minutes and one survey release, not a complete picture of the Fed's reaction function. The minutes describe views as of late September, before the weak September jobs report and before the latest public comments from Jefferson and Williams. The source is a secondary trade publication summarizing primary documents, so nuance may be lost. What to watch next: whether the October jobs and inflation data confirm the softening trend, whether consumer inflation expectations continue to rise, and whether the Fed's year-end projection of one more hike survives contact with incoming information.
