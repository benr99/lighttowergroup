Instinct's $1B Series C at a $10B valuation shows the speed of consumer AI agent fervor

A month after a $2.5 billion valuation, Instinct has raised another $1 billion from Sequoia Capital, Benchmark Capital and Coatue, valuing the company at $10 bi

Ben Rohr September 28, 2026 2 min read 1 source
Share LinkedIn X / Twitter

Instinct, an AI assistant startup that launched its invite-only service in August 2026, has raised a $1 billion Series C at a $10 billion valuation. The round, confirmed in a press release on Monday, comes only about a month after the company announced a fundraise that valued it at $2.5 billion. Investors in the new round include Sequoia Capital, Benchmark Capital and Coatue. The speed of the revaluation—from $2.5 billion to $10 billion in roughly a month—matters because it signals intense investor competition for a new class of consumer AI agents that can act on a user's behalf, not just answer questions.

The mechanics of the round are notable for what is and is not disclosed. Instinct confirmed the Series C after The Information reported the fundraising efforts earlier in September 2026. The company has not shared user numbers or growth metrics. Founder Noah Shinn declined interviews alongside the fundraising news, but a statement attributed to him said: "We're building Instinct to be the best personal agent that can handle the deeply personal nuances of everyday life. This funding helps us bring Instinct to more people and continue building the future of personal AI. It's an exciting, creative time, and we're just getting started." The absence of disclosed operating metrics means the $10 billion valuation rests on investor conviction rather than publicly verifiable traction.

The evidence base for this analysis is a single TechCrunch article read in full, published on September 28, 2026. The report describes Instinct's capabilities: when asked to perform a task, Instinct uses its own phone number and computer. The company has rolled out features including a "concierge" that can make phone calls for users, such as managing appointments at places without online booking, and a "trusted person network" that lets one person's Instinct agent coordinate plans with friends' agents. The article also notes that Instinct communicates with users via SMS and texting and does not yet have a mobile app. These details come from one secondary source, so the analysis must stay close to that reporting and avoid broader market claims not present in the dossier.

The sector implications are twofold. First, the rapid valuation increase reflects broader fervor around consumer AI agents that can complete tasks such as booking travel, making purchases, paying bills, canceling subscriptions, conducting research and ordering groceries. Second, competition is already intensifying. Meta's AI assistant, Muse, offers many similar features and can deeply integrate with Meta's social products, including monitoring and summarizing Instagram DMs or Facebook Groups and tracking Marketplace listings. TechCrunch reports that Muse has reached the top of U.S. app stores and has been downloaded millions of times. Instinct's lack of a mobile app could be a structural disadvantage against a competitor embedded in Meta's distribution network.

Several limitations and open questions remain. Some users are questioning how much personal information they must disclose to AI agents to access these capabilities. Instinct's initial privacy policy was described as particularly worrisome due to its overreach, though the policy has since been updated. The company has not disclosed user numbers, growth metrics, revenue, or the specific terms of the Series C beyond the amount and valuation. What to watch next is whether Instinct can sustain its valuation without a mobile app, how it responds to privacy concerns, and whether Meta's Muse erodes its early viral adoption.
