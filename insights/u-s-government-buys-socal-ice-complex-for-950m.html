U.S. Government Buys SoCal ICE Complex for $950M

The U.S. government has acquired a three-property immigration detention complex in Southern California's Inland Empire for $950 million, marking a significant expansion of the Department of Homeland Security's real estate footprint. GEO Group, the Boca Raton, Fla.-based prison operator, sold the assets to the federal government for use by U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement. The transaction matters because it converts privately owned detention infrastructure into government-owned assets while preserving GEO's operational role, a structure that could reshape how private prison companies manage federal immigration facilities.

The sale includes two ICE processing centers in Adelanto, San Bernardino County, with 1,280 beds and 660 beds respectively, plus the 704-bed Desert View Annex. In total, the Department of Homeland Security acquired 2,644 beds. GEO expects to net roughly $705 million after taxes, transaction fees, and other expenses. The company said it plans to use proceeds, along with operating cash flow, to reduce debt, repurchase shares, and fund general corporate purposes. Simultaneously, GEO's board increased its share-buyback authorization by $750 million to $1.25 billion, effective through the end of 2029.

The evidence comes from a single Commercial Observer report published October 5, 2026, by Greg Cornfield. The report states that GEO is set to continue providing support services at all three facilities under its existing contract with ICE, which runs through December 2029 and includes a five-year option to extend through December 2034. GEO also said it is in discussions to sell multiple other company-owned facilities to ICE, with GEO continuing to manage the properties under long-term contracts. The report notes that Commercial Observer earlier reported DHS and ICE have been negotiating office leases as well as acquisitions and leases of large detention uses, indicating a broader agency strategy.

For the commercial real estate and private corrections sectors, the transaction signals that federal agencies are willing to buy detention assets outright rather than rely solely on private ownership. This could create a pipeline of sale-leaseback-like arrangements where operators such as GEO monetize real estate while retaining service contracts. GEO owns or provides services at 97 facilities totaling approximately 76,000 beds worldwide, suggesting additional dispositions could follow. The $950 million price for 2,644 beds implies a per-bed valuation of roughly $359,000, though the dossier does not provide a breakdown by property or confirm whether the price reflects only real estate or includes other considerations.

Several limitations apply. The dossier is based on a single full-text source, and key details remain unknown: the closing date, the allocation of proceeds between debt reduction and buybacks, the tax treatment beyond the net figure, and whether the $950 million price includes any premium for the existing ICE contract. The report does not specify whether the transaction was a direct sale or part of a broader appropriation, nor does it provide independent confirmation from DHS or ICE. Future reporting should watch for additional facility sales to ICE, the exercise of the five-year contract option through 2034, and whether GEO's share-buyback activity accelerates following the net proceeds of approximately $705 million.
