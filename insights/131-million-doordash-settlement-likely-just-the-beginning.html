$131 Million DoorDash Settlement Likely Just the Beginning

New York City's landmark $131.5 million settlement with DoorDash over minimum pay violations signals a new phase of algorithmic accountability for gig platforms

Ben Rohr October 06, 2026 3 min read 1 source
Share LinkedIn X / Twitter

New York City's $131.5 million settlement with DoorDash marks a turning point in the regulation of gig economy pay. The agreement, reached after the city found DoorDash violated a 2023 minimum pay law for food delivery workers, is the largest enforcement action of its kind. It matters because it confirms what workers and advocates have long suspected: that app companies can obscure or manipulate legally mandated pay, and that only sustained government oversight can hold them accountable. The settlement is likely just the beginning of an iterative enforcement process as the city builds new data capabilities to monitor the apps.

The underlying law mandated $17.96 an hour before tips, a rate that increases with inflation and now stands at $22.13. It was prompted by a 2022 report finding that New York City food delivery workers made around $14 an hour, about half of which came from tips. A central and contentious feature of the law is the requirement that workers be paid not only while actively delivering, but also while on-duty and waiting for a task—known as "on-call" time. That money goes into an aggregate pool that companies disburse to deliverers, but not necessarily to the specific worker who was waiting. DoorDash directed on-call money to higher-rated and top-performing workers, effectively using a legally mandated sum as a management mechanism. The company called this money a "bonus," a characterization the city rejected.

The Department of Consumer and Worker Protection estimates that approximately $70 million of the total worker relief is attributable to violations of the law's "aggregate" requirement, according to a DCWP spokesperson cited in the source. DoorDash claimed it "calculated on-call time differently than the City, and believe our approach was fair, practical, and legal," but nonetheless agreed to settle and make workers whole. The settlement follows a pattern of app companies adjusting their designs in response to regulation. When the pay law debuted, Uber and DoorDash changed their apps to force consumers to leave tips only after checkout, and average gratuity plummeted from $3-$4 per order to $1.12 in the last quarter of 2025. The City Council later passed a law to make the tip option more prominent again, which went into effect this year.

The broader implications extend beyond DoorDash. The city has established a new research and analytics division at DCWP that can mine data for corporate violations, and the settlement is expected to give that team access to more DoorDash data. This kind of oversight is far beyond the ability of individual workers, who face faceless algorithms that obscure information or change quickly. The source notes that more than 60% of New York City food delivery workers land below the federal poverty line, according to a recent survey, underscoring the stakes. The apps also pushed workers to accept shifts of just a few hours, making it harder to log on and earn a living wage even with the minimum pay law in place.

The evidence base for this analysis is a single full-text source from CityLand NYC, a publication focused on New York City land use and regulatory matters. The article relies on a DCWP spokesperson for the $70 million aggregate-violation estimate and on a survey for the poverty statistic, but the underlying survey methodology and full settlement terms are not detailed in the dossier. What remains unknown is how DoorDash will disburse the funds, whether other delivery apps face similar scrutiny, and how the new DCWP data team will use the information obtained through this enforcement action. The source itself frames the outcome as an iterative process, suggesting that further back-and-forth between the city and the apps is likely.
