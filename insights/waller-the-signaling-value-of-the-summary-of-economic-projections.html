Waller, The Signaling Value of the Summary of Economic Projections

Governor Christopher Waller framed the FOMC's September rate increase as the culmination of a year-long shift in risks, not a reaction to a single data point, a

Ben Rohr October 08, 2026 3 min read 1 source
Share LinkedIn X / Twitter

Governor Christopher J. Waller used an October 8, 2026 speech in Istanbul to explain why the Federal Open Market Committee raised its policy rate by 25 basis points in September, to a range of 3.75 percent to 4 percent, after nine months on hold. The speech matters because it addresses the central question facing markets and the public after a policy turn: what changed, and what comes next. Waller's answer is that the September decision was not a sharp reversal driven by one or two data points, but the culmination of factors that developed over the preceding year, including a stabilizing labor market and persistent inflationary forces.

The mechanics of the shift are laid out in the speech. The FOMC had cut rates by 75 basis points over three successive meetings in the second half of 2025, when inflation was fairly close to the 2 percent target after accounting for tariff effects and when labor market weakness appeared to skew risks toward employment. Waller described those cuts as insurance against an economic slowdown. By the first half of 2026, however, the labor market appeared to stabilize while progress on inflation stalled. Core personal consumption expenditures inflation moderated to 0.1 percent in June and an initial estimate of 0.2 percent in July, later revised to 0.1 percent, but Waller said other forces undermined faith in that progress.

The evidence Waller cited includes fading hopes for a quick end to the Middle East conflict, with experts warning that low inventories and damaged infrastructure could keep oil prices high through 2027. He also pointed to mounting evidence that the artificial intelligence buildout was significantly driving up high-tech consumer prices, and to continuing trade conflicts that threatened new tariffs. Waller said these forces became clearer as the year passed, swamping fleeting signs of progress toward 2 percent inflation. When the first August inflation reading came in hot just before the September meeting, he concluded that inflation was still too high and not making sufficient progress toward the target. The speech is a single primary source from the Federal Reserve, so the analysis is bounded by Waller's own account and does not include independent corroboration of the economic data he describes.

For markets, the speech's most important implication is that the September increase was a judgment about the balance of risks, not a one-off response to a consumer price index print. Waller explicitly rejected the suggestion that policy shifted based largely on a single data point, saying it was a preponderance of evidence over several months that led him to conclude the policy setting maintained from December 2025 through September 2026 would not return inflation to 2 percent in a timely manner. That framing suggests the FOMC's reaction function is now more sensitive to persistent inflationary forces, including energy supply constraints, AI-related price pressures, and tariff risks, than to short-term monthly volatility in core inflation.

The speech's limitations are notable. The published text is truncated, so Waller's full discussion of the Summary of Economic Projections and his forward guidance on the policy path is not available in the dossier. The evidence level is single full text, meaning there is no independent verification of the labor market stabilization, the AI price effects, or the expert warnings about oil prices through 2027. What to watch next is whether subsequent FOMC communications and data releases corroborate Waller's characterization of a strengthened labor market and persistent inflationary forces, and whether the Summary of Economic Projections signals a higher-for-longer policy path consistent with his account.
