HBT Financial Buys Its Way to the $10B Cliff, Shedding Mortgage Risk

The $204.6M Tri-County deal pushes pro-forma assets to $8.3B, forcing the regulatory threshold conversation now.

Ben Rohr August 11, 2026 3 min read 1 source

HBT Financial’s $204.6 million agreement to buy Tri-County Financial Group will push the Bloomington, Illinois-based bank to roughly $8.3 billion in pro forma assets — close enough to the $10 billion regulatory threshold that the costs of crossing it are now a live part of the investment case. The deal, HBT’s twelfth acquisition since 2007 and its second in ten months, deliberately strips out the one piece of the target that would make that threshold even more painful: a money-losing mortgage banking operation that will be sold or shuttered before closing.

Under the terms announced Monday, Tri-County shareholders may elect to receive 2.4589 shares of HBT common stock, $71.01 in cash, or a combination for each share they hold. At close, expected in the first quarter of 2027, Tri-County owners will hold about 9% of the combined company. HBT, parent of Heartland Bank and Trust Company, gains $1.3 billion in loans and an equal amount of deposits across 19 branches in central and northern Illinois, while assuming the costs — and the regulatory clock — that come with becoming a much larger institution.

The acquisition calculus turns on how much of Tri-County’s noninterest expense base can be removed. HBT is targeting expense savings equal to 34% of those costs, excluding the mortgage unit, with 80% of the cuts phasing in during 2027. The plan also acknowledges the physical footprint: five First State Bank branches sit within two miles of existing Heartland offices, and Chief Financial Officer Peter Chapman told American Banker that consolidation will occur “where it’s logical to do so.” HBT estimates $19 million in pretax transaction expenses.

Brendan Nosal, an analyst at Hovde Group, pointed to the mortgage divestiture as a net positive, writing that he takes “no issue” with eliminating a large, loss-producing operation and the accompanying gain-on-sale earnings volatility. Yet he also noted that with pro forma assets at $8.3 billion, questions about the $10 billion threshold “are now a valid part of the conversation.” The six-billion-dollar bank buying its way toward the ten-billion-dollar cliff just made sure not to carry a money-losing mortgage unit up the slope.

That threshold matters because it triggers new regulatory challenges, including the Durbin amendment’s cap on debit interchange fees. By buying closer to the line now and discarding the non-interest income source that would exaggerate the earnings hit, HBT is effectively pre-paying some of the regulatory cost of crossing — using scale to fund the transition rather than letting the deadline arrive unmanaged. The risk, of course, is that the integration falters, the cost cuts prove too aggressive, or the deposit base erodes as overlapping branches close.

For Tri-County shareholders, the deal provides a classic exit at a premium, with the election to take cash or stock giving them a choice between liquidity and continued exposure to HBT’s post-acquisition performance. Chairman Tom Prescott will join HBT’s board, and CEO Kirk Ross will become a senior management officer at Heartland Bank and Trust — retaining some continuity but placing the execution burden squarely on HBT.

The transaction arrives amid a fitful year for bank M&A.; Through July, 102 deals had been announced, roughly in line with the prior year’s pace, as M&A; activity slowed after the start of the U.S.-Iran war, according to Seaport Research Partners. HBT’s willingness to do a second deal in under a year, and to absorb a target that brings it within arm’s reach of a regulatory cliff, suggests management sees more value in scaling through the threshold than in staying comfortably below it.

What remains unanswered is whether HBT will accelerate further acquisitions to push well past $10 billion, thereby diluting the proportional impact of the interchange cap, or whether it will pause and digest. The answer will appear in the pace of branch consolidation, the realized cost saves, and the bank’s next capital raise.
