UCLA Buyer Picks Up a West LA Co-Living Asset From Receivership

The $8.5M sale at $472 per square foot tests whether a student-housing conversion can cover the basis.

Ben Rohr August 15, 2026 2 min read 1 source
Share LinkedIn X / Twitter

One chart, one argument

The building is barely three years old. 1775 Beloit Avenue, a seven-story, 18,000-square-foot co-living property in West Los Angeles, sold for $8,500,000 through a court-appointed receivership. Colliers' Kitty Wallace represented both sides: the buyer, UCLA Housing Group, and the seller, Enterprise Bank. The buyer plans to convert the 16-unit, 48-bed project into student housing.

The price does the talking: $531,250 per unit, roughly $472 per square foot, and about $177,083 per bed. That last number is the most useful. Student housing is priced by bed, not by unit, and the buyer is paying for 48 beds near a major university at a basis that Wallace frames as below replacement cost, without quantifying the benchmark.

The seller's position is clearer: Enterprise Bank sold through receivership, meaning it was not marketing a performing, fully leased asset. It was resolving something that had gone wrong enough to reach court supervision. The available sale summary leaves out the prior loan balance, the occupancy, the rental rate, and what the bank recovered. What remains visible is the exit: a clean sale to a buyer who could close without the old co-living story having to work first.

The timing matters. The property delivered in 2023, just as Los Angeles multifamily rent growth was muted, per Wallace. Co-living delivered attainable beds near employment and education centers, but the post-pandemic rent environment compressed the margin between operating income and the cost of capital. A project that opened into that pressure was vulnerable, and a receiver sale is the visible residue.

UCLA Housing Group's bet is that student demand is more predictable than the open-market co-living renter. But the conversion is not free, and the reported terms do not quantify the cost to change the layout, operations, or leasing structure from co-living to student housing, or whether the buyer will pay cash or finance the basis. At 18,000 square feet, even a modest conversion budget would move the all-in per-bed cost materially.

No cap rate, DSCR, loan assumption, or occupancy figure appears in the sale announcement. This is not a financing story; it is a basis story with a receivership forcing function. The buyer paid $8.5 million for a physical asset near UCLA at a price that no longer depends on the co-living operating model succeeding. The open question is whether student housing operations can produce enough per-bed rent to make that basis work after conversion.
