CVC’s $9.3 Billion Secondaries Fund: A Test of Scale

CVC Capital Partners closed its sixth secondaries fund at $9.3 billion, blowing past an $8 billion target and marking a 37% jump from its predecessor. The final tally confirms the ferocious institutional appetite for secondaries as a liquidity valve in a slow exit environment, yet the sheer scale of the vehicle now presents a deployment puzzle that could erode the very return profile that attracted limited partners in the first place.

LPs rushed to commit, eager for exposure to a strategy that has thrived amid a logjam of aging portfolio assets. But a $9.3 billion fund must swallow larger and more complex transactions to put capital to work within a reasonable timeframe. The secondaries market, while growing, remains capacity-constrained: annual volumes hover around $100–$120 billion, and a handful of mega-funds now compete for the same GP-led continuation vehicles and large LP portfolio sales. CVC’s new pool alone represents roughly 8–9% of a typical year’s total deal volume. The firm will have to write equity checks of $500 million or more per deal—a scale that narrows the universe of actionable opportunities.

The predecessor fund, which closed at approximately $6.8 billion, already operated at the upper end of the market. The step-up to $9.3 billion forces CVC to either participate in ever-larger syndicates or lead deals that require underwriting steep leverage or complex structures. Both paths introduce execution risk. In a GP-led deal, for instance, a sponsor selling a single asset or a strip of assets to itself in a continuation vehicle often demands a premium valuation. The buying secondaries fund must then rely on future operational improvements to generate a return, rather than the classic discount-to-NAV entry that characterized the strategy’s earlier vintages. If CVC pays up for quality assets to absorb its capital, gross returns may compress.

CVC itself gains a massive new fee stream—management fees alone on $9.3 billion will add a nine-figure annual revenue line—but the reputational risk of underperformance looms large. LPs in secondaries funds increasingly scrutinize net IRRs and DPI, not just headline closes. A fund that struggles to deploy or that produces a sub-1.5x MOIC after a decade will damage the GP’s standing in a core growth area. The firm has not disclosed the strategy’s target returns, but industry benchmarks for large secondaries funds hover in the mid-teens net IRR. Achieving that on a $9.3 billion base requires significant alpha generation from deal selection and operational value creation.

The precise composition of the LP base remains unreported, though large secondaries funds typically draw sovereign wealth funds, public pensions, and insurers. Their time horizon matches the illiquid nature of the asset class, but they will expect CVC to maintain pricing discipline. The next signal to watch is the pace and nature of the first few investments. If CVC announces a string of billion-dollar-plus continuation vehicle deals within the first year, it will be a clear sign that scale is driving strategy. The market will then calculate the implied entry multiples and judge whether the firm is buying return-challenged assets at full prices. For now, the oversubscription is a testament to CVC’s brand, but the real test begins when the capital starts moving.
