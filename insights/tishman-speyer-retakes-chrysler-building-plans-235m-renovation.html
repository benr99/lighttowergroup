Tishman Speyer Retakes Chrysler Building, Plans $235M Renovation

Tishman Speyer has signed a 150-year ground lease for the Chrysler Building, agreeing to pay $235M plus future ground lease payments, and plans to reposition th

Ben Rohr October 08, 2026 3 min read 1 source
Share LinkedIn X / Twitter

Tishman Speyer has regained control of the Chrysler Building, one of Manhattan's most recognizable art deco towers, after signing a 150-year ground lease with Cooper Union. The deal matters because it returns a distressed trophy asset to an experienced operator at a moment when the surrounding Grand Central submarket is posting the highest direct asking rents in Manhattan. The firm agreed to pay $235M plus future ground lease payments, the amounts of which were not disclosed, and plans to reposition the 77-story skyscraper at 405 Lexington Ave. as a 1.3M SF boutique office tower using 100% equity from a group of institutional investors led by the Public Sector Pension Investment Board.

The mechanics of the transaction trace back to a chain of ownership failures. RFR and Austrian firm Signa Holding GmbH bought the tower in 2019 for just over $150M, eleven years after the Abu Dhabi Investment Council paid $800M for a 90% stake. Tishman Speyer had previously paid $220M for the ground lease in 1997 and sold its 10% stake to RFR. Escalating rent payments to Cooper Union—from $7.8M in 2018 to a high of $32.5M—were the main cause of the building's deteriorating value. After Signa filed for insolvency in November 2023, RFR and Signa stopped paying rent and could not complete planned renovations, leading Cooper Union to begin eviction proceedings in September 2024. A judge sided with the university the following January, and Cushman & Wakefield has managed the property since then.

The evidence comes from a single full-text Bisnow New York report, which includes commentary from Savills Vice Chairman David Heller, who represented Cooper Union alongside Mark Todrys. Heller said there was "a tremendous amount of interest" in the bidding war and added, "There's only one Chrysler Building." He also noted that roughly 50% of the building is vacant and that Tishman and its partners plan to start work immediately. The renovation plan includes turning 75% of vacant or soon-to-be-vacant space into prebuilt office suites, converting the 61st floor into an amenity space with a clubhouse, outdoor terrace, and food and beverage offerings, and using space below the lobby for wellness services. The building's exterior, including its signature crown, will be polished and refreshed.

The market context supports the repositioning thesis. The Bisnow report cites Colliers data showing Manhattan is on pace for the most office leases signed since 2000, with virtually no availability in best-in-class towers. Developers including SL Green, BXP, and Extell Development are embarking on new office developments, while millions of square feet have been taken offline for residential conversions. Avison Young data shows the Grand Central submarket had the highest direct asking rents of all Manhattan submarkets in the third quarter at $113.45 per SF. Tishman Speyer CEO Rob Speyer framed the move as opportunistic: "We are pursuing an ambitious plan for the Chrysler Building at a historically strong moment for the Manhattan office market."

Several limitations temper the analysis. The source does not disclose the future ground lease payment amounts, the total renovation budget beyond the $235M lease payment, the identity of all institutional investors beyond PSP Investments, or the projected timeline for delivery. The report also does not provide current occupancy figures beyond Heller's estimate of roughly 50% vacancy, nor does it detail how the prebuilt suite strategy will compete with newer boutique office product in the submarket. The deterioration described by tenants in 2024—cracks in the ceiling and rodents in ground-floor retail—suggests the renovation scope may be larger than the disclosed amenity and prebuilt plans imply. What to watch is whether Tishman Speyer can lease the repositioned space at rents sufficient to cover the undisclosed ground lease obligations while preserving Cooper Union's permanent financial support for the school.
