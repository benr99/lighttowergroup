SEC Charges South Florida Resident and His Company for Alleged Investment Scheme Defrauding Law Enforcement

The SEC alleges CMI Capital LLC and Michael D. Williams raised roughly $860,000 from at least 18 investors, many current or retired law enforcement officers, us

Ben Rohr September 24, 2026 3 min read 1 source
Share LinkedIn X / Twitter

The Securities and Exchange Commission has charged CMI Capital LLC and its founder and manager, Michael D. Williams, with an alleged fraudulent investment scheme that raised approximately $860,000 from at least 18 investors, many of whom are current or retired law enforcement officers in South Florida. The case matters because it shows how a professional relationship with a third-party police and firefighter pension plan administrator can be leveraged to build trust with a specific occupational community, and because the alleged misconduct spans multiple federal securities laws, including antifraud and registration provisions of the Securities Act of 1933, the Securities Exchange Act of 1934, and the Investment Advisers Act of 1940.

According to the SEC's complaint, from at least October 2023 through August 2024, Williams made numerous false and misleading statements to convince clients to invest in two funds he controlled. Investors were allegedly told that one of the funds had a portfolio value of more than $5 million and achieved returns exceeding 140 percent. The SEC alleges that one tactic used to support those claims was sending cropped screenshots of graphics that showed exorbitant trading profits. Williams allegedly misappropriated approximately $384,000 of investor and client funds to pay for personal expenses, including credit card balances, a sports car, and vacations. The complaint also states that Williams began repaying certain investors in August 2024 and has repaid more than $375,000 to certain investors.

The evidence base for this article is a single primary source: the SEC press release published on September 23, 2026. The release quotes Stephanie N. Moot, Director of the SEC's Miami Regional Office, who said the defendants allegedly used cropped screenshots to trick investors and urged investors to be wary of anyone promising high returns with little to no risk. The defendants have agreed to a bifurcated settlement in connection with the charges, subject to court approval. Without admitting the allegations, they consented to entry of judgments that would permanently enjoin them from violating the charged provisions and further enjoin Williams from participating in the issuance, purchase, offer, or sale of any security except for certain transactions in his personal accounts. The proposed judgments also provide for disgorgement with prejudgment interest against Williams and civil penalties against the defendants in amounts to be determined by the court. Williams also agreed to a forthcoming associational bar.

For the banking and credit sector, the case is a reminder that investment adviser fraud can emerge through adjacent trust networks rather than through traditional banking channels. The alleged use of a pension plan administrator role to access law enforcement clients highlights a vulnerability in occupational affinity fraud, where shared professional identity lowers skepticism. The registration charges also signal that the SEC is willing to pursue unregistered offering activity alongside antifraud claims, which may increase compliance attention for small funds and advisers operating in regional markets. The fact that the alleged scheme involved only two funds and fewer than two dozen investors shows that enforcement attention is not limited to large-scale cases.

The main limitation of this analysis is that the SEC's allegations remain unproven in court, and the defendants have not admitted the claims. The press release does not provide the names of the two funds, the specific dates of individual misstatements, the exact number of law enforcement investors, or how the cropped screenshots were distributed. It also does not state whether the $375,000 in repayments came from investor funds or other sources, nor does it detail the status of the remaining investor losses. What to watch next is whether the court approves the proposed judgments, what disgorgement and civil penalty amounts are ultimately ordered, and whether any parallel criminal or state regulatory actions emerge.
