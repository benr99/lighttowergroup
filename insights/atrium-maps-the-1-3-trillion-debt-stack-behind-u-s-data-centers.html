Atrium Maps the $1.3 Trillion Debt Stack Behind U.S. Data Centers

Regional utilities carry the largest reported credit slice—$428 billion—while $128 billion matures by 2027.

Ben Rohr August 18, 2026 3 min read 1 source
Share LinkedIn X / Twitter

One chart, one argument

The largest credit bucket in U.S. data center development is not a hyperscaler bond program or a private credit fund. It is the regional utility. Atrium’s “Who Finances America’s Data Centers” platform puts utility syndicated credit facilities at $428 billion across 27 firms, with the top 10 accounting for 68 percent of that total. PG&E; leads at $64.6 billion, followed by Southern Company’s $27.7 billion and Dominion Energy’s $26 billion.

The San Diego-based AI analytics startup, founded in 2024, combined county filings, SEC credit agreements, CMBS securitizations, syndicated loan data, and corporate debt to cover 3,038 operational data center deals and 1,258 earlier-stage assets—nearly 4,300 U.S. data centers in total. After isolating $93.1 billion of overlap, the platform attributes roughly $1.1 trillion of the $1.3 trillion identifiable debt stack to property mortgages, syndicated facilities, private credit, hyperscaler corporate debt, and utility borrowings.

Property mortgages account for $148 billion, with Wells Fargo at $8.2 billion and JPMorgan Chase at $6.9 billion. The broader lender view shows the importance of syndicated participation: TD Bank’s $14.8 billion in direct loans jumps to $27 billion once 79 syndications are included, making it the largest data center lender in the analysis; Wells Fargo and JPMorgan follow at $18.7 billion and $17.9 billion.

Syndicated facilities total $224 billion. The largest individual syndications include Coreweave’s $23 billion facility from 38 lenders, DigitalBridge and IFM’s $20 billion from 24 lenders, QTS and Blackstone’s $18.5 billion from 24 lenders, and Equinix’s $16.2 billion from 31 lenders.

Private credit is concentrated but growing. PIMCO reported $23 billion of originations, $18 billion of which came from the October 2025 Meta and Blue Owl Beignet bond issuance. Blue Owl Capital and Blackstone Credit follow at $11 billion and $8 billion. Across SPV and private credit structures, the platform counts $158 billion. Across the lender rankings, three of the top 15 are nonbanks—PIMCO, Blue Owl, and Blackstone Credit—whereas two years ago none would have appeared.

The hyperscalers carry $223 billion in long-term debt and credit facilities. Amazon leads at $67.2 billion, including $32 billion of 2025 data center capital expenditures; Microsoft is next at $53.8 billion, including $44 billion of capex. The big five hyperscalers account for $600 billion in 2026 capital expenditures, up 36 percent year over year; the platform’s risk scenario is that a 20 percent cut by any single player would propagate through construction, equipment, and build-to-suit developers.

The maturity schedule is the clearest refinancing pressure point. The platform finds $128 billion in data center debt maturing between 2025 and 2027, exceeding the entire U.S. office CMBS maturity wall in that window, and the figure rises to $213 billion through 2029. Much of that debt was originated when SOFR sat between 0.05 percent and 4.5 percent; refinancing today is above 6 percent, with leveraged borrowers seeing 8 percent prints.

The constraint is also physical. PJM Interconnection forecasts 30 gigawatts of incremental data center demand by 2030, equivalent to 30 nuclear reactors. Atrium warns that by June 2027 the grid could fall below reliability standards, and that keeping it adequate could add $100 billion in consumer electricity costs across 65 million people within seven years. That political friction is already visible: Virginia hosts 668 data centers, 56 percent more than second-place Texas, and Loudoun County alone hosts 306 facilities—a facility count higher than the total in more than 45 U.S. states—as state lawmakers propose restrictions. The platform counts 48 national developments worth $158 billion canceled in 2025; by early 2026, the number of blocked or delayed projects had grown to 75, representing $130 billion.

The unresolved question is whether this debt is a bridge to contracted power or a bet on demand that has not yet cleared. Atrium has made the money visible; the grid will decide how much of it gets repaid.
